Living with the Plant

The plant was the area's anchor employer. A 1984 Energy Department report put its permanent workforce at 9,200 in December 1982, most of them working for Du Pont, with plant workers and their families making up about half of Aiken's 15,000 people.

The same report found that Aiken and Richmond counties ranked in the top half nationally for income in 1974, while most counties around the plant ranked in the bottom 11 percent (IA: DOE, 1984).

The 1980s buildup brought the workforce to 18,635 by May 1989, and a 1990 DOE study said shutting the K, L and P reactors would cost about 9,600 jobs (IA: DOE, 1990). In 1992 South Carolina listed Westinghouse Savannah River, with 23,000 workers, as its largest manufacturing employer, and noted that federal cutbacks had begun (IA: South Carolina Economic Report, 1993).

The plant's neighbors and workers also raised questions about their health. In August 1989 Senator Glenn argued that DOE's research on the health of its own workers had been subordinated to production. Senator Kohl called for removing the apparent conflict of interest, and said he was open to moving that research to Health and Human Services.

The record included a 1988 study that reported a significant excess of leukemia deaths among hourly Savannah River workers hired before 1955 (IA: Senate hearing, 1989). The later dose reconstruction was run by the CDC (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention) (WB: CDC).

Studies of the neighbors began at the same time. A July 1990 National Institutes of Health survey compared cancer deaths in Aiken and Barnwell counties and Georgia's Burke County with similar counties elsewhere. It found no excess among children, more leukemia at ages 20 to 39 and slightly more lung cancer in older adults, and said it did not show cancer excesses tied to the plant (DL; DL).

Some of those worries had already turned into public protest, told in An Emerging Environmental Movement Impacts SRS.
