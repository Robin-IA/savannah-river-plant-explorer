An Emerging Environmental Movement Impacts SRS

A new environmental movement changed the rules for the plant. The National Environmental Policy Act, signed in January 1970, required federal projects to study their effects on the environment in public impact statements (WB: SRS at Fifty, pp. 474–75).

In December 1970 a reorganization created the Environmental Protection Agency, which took over the federal journal that reprinted the plant's radiation monitoring (DL). In December 1974 the EPA issued the plant its first water-discharge permit (WB: SRS at Fifty, pp. 480–81).

By the late 1970s many environmentalists had also turned against nuclear power. Near the plant their anger focused on two projects: a plant to recycle plutonium from power-reactor fuel at Barnwell, built on land cut from the site's eastern edge, and Georgia Power's planned nuclear plant at Waynesboro, Georgia (WB: SRS at Fifty, pp. 482–83).

The Three Mile Island accident in Pennsylvania on March 28, 1979 (DL) gave the movement new force. Small protests at Savannah River had come and gone through the decade; the largest came that year. About 1,500 people joined a three-day protest of seminars, marches and a concert by Graham Nash.

It ended with 162 arrests for trespassing at the plant, the Barnwell plant and a nearby radioactive-waste dump, 30 of them at Savannah River (WB: SRS at Fifty, p. 482). The site dates its years of anti-nuclear protest from then (WB: srs.gov 1970s).

The Reagan-era buildup brought the protests to the plant's gates. In late May 1982, 500 to 700 people rallied near the plant for a freeze on nuclear weapons (IA: Florida Flambeau, June 3, 1982).

The plant also reached a national audience on film. Building Bombs (1989), made by Mark Mori and Susan Robinson, is a film about the environmental legacy and social impact of the Savannah River Plant, “the nation’s largest manufacturer of hydrogen bomb materials during the Cold War”, in the words of PBS’s POV (WB).

It was nominated for the Academy Award for Best Documentary Feature at the 63rd Academy Awards in March 1991 (WB: oscars.org). Public television did not show it at first.

In 1992 the cable series Alternative Views presented the film with an interview with its co-director, saying PBS had refused to broadcast it (IA: Alternative Views #466). After three years of argument and a campaign Mori organized, an edited version, retitled Building Bombs: The Legacy, aired on POV on August 10, 1993 (WB: Los Angeles Times).

These protests ran alongside the official challenges told elsewhere in this chapter: the lawsuit over restarting L reactor (L-Reactor comes back) and the investigations of 1988 (1987–1988: the reactors stop).

The same years brought the damage to the site's land and wildlife into the open, as SRS and Ecology tells.
