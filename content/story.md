*A draft, told from the record wherever it is held. Sources checked live
6 November 2026.*

## Act 1. Building the bomb plant, 1950–1969

### A site for the hydrogen bomb

In June 1950, with war begun in Korea, the Atomic Energy Commission enlarged
the job it was asking Du Pont to take on: three more heavy-water reactors,
five in all ([WB: *SRS at Fifty* ch. 7][fifty-7]). Du Pont surveyed candidate
sites and settled on the middle Savannah River in South Carolina, a tract big
enough to hold an exclusion zone around the reactors
([WB: ch. 7][fifty-7]). DOE's own timeline dates President Truman's letter to
Du Pont to 20 October 1950 ([EXT: DOE, energy.gov/srs][doe-history]).

The announcement came at noon on 28 November 1950: about 250,000 acres in
Aiken and Barnwell counties, to be called the Savannah River Plant, designed,
built and operated by Du Pont ([WB: *SRS at Fifty* ch. 8][fifty-8]). The next
morning it was in the *Atlanta Constitution*
([IA](https://archive.org/details/per_atlanta-constitution_1950-11-29_83_142))
and, by wire, in papers as far away as Victoria, British Columbia
([IA](https://archive.org/details/victoriadailytimes1950-11-29)).

### The towns inside the fence

The land was not empty. Ellenton, with about 600 people, and Dunbarton, with
231, stood inside the boundary, along with Meyers Mill, Jackson, Hawthorne,
Robbins, Leigh, Snelling and Hattieville ([WB: ch. 7][fifty-7]). Most of the
farms were worked by tenants, and most of the agricultural workforce was
African American ([WB: ch. 7][fifty-7]).

How many people were moved depends on who is counting. The first press
release spoke of about 1,500 families; early reports said up to 8,000
farmers; the AEC settled on 6,000 people in 1,500 families
([WB: ch. 8][fifty-8]). The Savannah River Archaeological Research Program
later put it at about 5,000 ([WB: SRARP][srarp-towns]).

The Army Corps of Engineers ran the acquisition. Residents of the first area
had to be gone by 15 March 1951 and of the second by 1 June; owners who
disputed an appraisal went to federal condemnation proceedings; at least
4,500 graves had to be moved ([WB: ch. 8][fifty-8]). By late 1951 the
government was acquiring 265 tracts in Ellenton alone
([DL](https://archive.org/details/sim_united-states-congress-hearings-prints-and-reports_1951-11-27)).
An information meeting in Ellenton drew more than 500 people, and the
photograph of it suggests it was segregated ([WB: ch. 8][fifty-8]).

*The Reporter* told the national story in March 1951 as the quiet doom of
Ellenton ([IA](https://archive.org/details/sim_reporter_1951-03-06_4_5)).
*Jet* told a different part of it that December: Black families moving
whole houses fifteen miles to New Ellenton, the replacement town
([IA](https://archive.org/details/sim_jet_1951-12-20_1_8)). A 1965 federal
housing study counted about 30 white and 120 Black families relocated near
New Ellenton ([IA](https://archive.org/details/impactoflargeins0000unse)),
and by 1953 Congress was hearing that New Ellenton had been left out of aid
it needed
([DL](https://archive.org/details/sim_united-states-congress-hearings-prints-and-reports_june-16-17-22-and-23-1953)).

The displaced spoke for themselves decades later, in oral histories
collected from 1990 and published as *Memories of Home* for Ellenton and for
Dunbarton and Meyers Mill ([EXT: OSTI 272945][osti-ellenton];
[EXT: OSTI 10171577][osti-dunbarton]). The program's web pages and the
chapter PDFs survive in Wayback; srarp.org itself is gone
([WB][srarp-towns]; [WB: Ellenton volume][srarp-ellenton]). James
McBride's life of James Brown touches on the Black sharecroppers forced out
of Ellenton ([IA](https://archive.org/details/killemleavesearc0000mcbr)).
Louise Cassels's *The Unexpected Exodus* (1971), the classic memoir of the
leaving, is not in the Archive ([EXT: Open Library][cassels]).

### Building it

Construction began in 1951 — the R reactor in June, P in July, L in October,
by the Natural Resources Defense Council's count
([IA](https://archive.org/details/usnuclearwarhead0002unse)). The Bureau of
Labor Statistics followed the work force in a four-part study in 1952
([IA: part I](https://archive.org/details/sim_monthly-labor-review_1952-06_74_6)).
It records Black tenant farmers arriving in Augusta looking for jobs in May
1951, and an NAACP meeting in Aiken that September charging Du Pont over its
hiring.

Housing the workers was its own crisis. Congress held a hearing in Aiken in
February 1951 that expected the construction peak around the start of 1953
([DL](https://archive.org/details/sim_united-states-congress-hearings-prints-and-reports_1951-02-28_0)),
and by February 1952 the mayors of New Ellenton and Augusta were testifying
about dormitories and Du Pont's part in housing
([DL](https://archive.org/details/sim_united-states-congress-hearings-prints-and-reports_1952-02-22_3)).
*House & Home* reported about 4,000 trailers for married workers
([IA](https://archive.org/details/usmodernist-HH-1953-08)). The housing
builder later sued Du Pont in a case that reached the Supreme Court
([DL](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40386417_2698)), and South
Carolina's attempt to tax Du Pont's work on the plant set out what a
government-owned, contractor-operated plant was in law
([DL](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385001_0003)).

Du Pont wrote its own record of all this, volume by volume, in construction
and design histories now on OSTI ([EXT: OSTI 10159481][osti-construction];
[EXT: OSTI 10151705][osti-plantwide]). The company's administrative files are
at the Hagley Museum and Library, by DOE's own finding aid
([EXT: DOE OHRE][ohre-admin]); Hagley's catalogue could not be checked
directly.

### Running it

The 305-M test pile was the first reactor to go critical on the site, in
1952, the year heavy-water production began in D Area
([WB: srs.gov 1950s][srs-1950]). R reactor went critical in December 1953, P
on 20 February 1954, and all five were running by the end of March 1955
([WB: *SRS at Fifty* ch. 11][fifty-11]). The F Area separations plant
started in 1954 and H Area in 1955; tritium went to the AEC from 1955; the
basic plant was complete in 1956 ([WB: srs.gov 1950s][srs-1950];
[WB: History Highlights][history1]). The first shipment of plutonium is
dated December 1954 by one official source and 1955 by others
([WB: ch. 11][fifty-11]; [WB][history1]).

In 1956 the neutrino was confirmed at P reactor. R and the heavy-water
components test reactor shut down in 1964, and L in 1968
([WB: srs.gov 1960s][srs-1960]). The standard history of the reactors is
Carlisle and Zenzen's *Supplying the Nuclear Arsenal*
([IA](https://archive.org/details/supplyingnuclear0000carl)), and the South
Carolina State Historic Preservation Office holds a long illustrated study of
the five reactor areas ([EXT: SC SHPO][shpo-reactors]).

### The ecologists arrive first

Before the reactors ran, the river was surveyed. Ruth Patrick of the Academy
of Natural Sciences made a baseline survey in 1951
([WB: srs.gov 1950s][srs-1950]), and the Public Health Service sampled the
river's water and its natural radioactivity in 1951–52
([IA](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41159405_0033);
[IA](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41159413_0274)). AEC officials
approached Eugene Odum of the University of Georgia in 1951 to study the
reservation's ecosystems
([IA](https://archive.org/details/bigecologyemerge0000cole);
[IA](https://archive.org/details/isbn_9780820324739)). That work became a
permanent laboratory in 1961 ([WB: srs.gov 1960s][srs-1960]) — the Savannah
River Ecology Laboratory, which dates its own founding to 1951
([WB: SREL][srel-radioecology]).

## Act 2. The reactors under scrutiny, 1970–1992

### A plant that measured itself

Through the 1970s the plant reported its releases. Du Pont's semiannual
monitoring summaries were reprinted in the federal *Radiation Data and
Reports* ([DL](https://archive.org/details/sim_radiation-data-and-reports_1970-12_11_12)),
and its annual environmental reports run from 1974 on OSTI
([EXT: OSTI 4174736][osti-env-1974]). In 1972 the plant became the first
National Environmental Research Park ([WB: srs.gov 1970s][srs-1970]). The
ecology laboratory's work on reactor cooling water began in 1969, once
satellite imagery had made the water temperatures impossible to keep secret
([EXT: Ecological Society of America][esa-thermal]), and became the
*Thermal Ecology* symposia
([IA](https://archive.org/details/thermalecologyii0000ther)). The first
on-site anti-nuclear protest came in 1979 ([WB: srs.gov 1970s][srs-1970]).

### L-Reactor comes back

The Reagan-era buildup needed more plutonium. In 1982 the plant lowered the
Pu-240 content of its product to blend with Hanford's
([WB: *SRS at Fifty* ch. 19][fifty-19]), and DOE moved to restart L reactor,
idle since 1968, on an environmental assessment rather than a full impact
statement ([EXT: OSTI 5060401][osti-l-ea]). The Natural Resources Defense
Council sued. On 15 July 1983 the district court held that the restart needed
an environmental impact statement but would not stop it
([EXT: Environmental Law Reporter][nrdc-vaughn]). The Senate Armed Services
Committee had already held a field hearing in North Augusta that February
([DL](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41152659_0884)). The final EIS came
in May 1984 ([EXT: DOE][l-eis]); it and the other impact statements of the
period are gathered in one archive.org upload
([IA](https://archive.org/details/SavannahRiverEnvironmentalImpactStatements)).
L restarted in 1985. C reactor shut down that year, permanently from 1986
([WB: srs.gov 1980s][srs-1980]).

Outside critics read the record differently from the operator. *Deadly Crop
in the Tank Farm* (1986) assessed the high-level waste tanks
([EXT: IEER][deadly-crop]); Du Pont published a rebuttal
([EXT: OSTI 5881523][osti-dupont-reply]). The *Washington Post* described
aging reactors carrying the buildup, one down for a cracked vessel — a
clipping that survives in the CIA's files
([IA](https://archive.org/details/cia-readingroom-document-cia-rdp88g01117r000401020009-5)).

### 1987–1988: the reactors stop

A National Academy of Sciences panel reviewed safety at the defense
production reactors for DOE in 1987
([IA](https://archive.org/details/ERIC_ED299108)). Du Pont announced that
year that it would not stay on as operator ([WB: srs.gov 1980s][srs-1980]).
In February 1988 a Senate subcommittee heard DOE, the plant manager and
Du Pont on why L reactor's power had been cut
([IA](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41152840_0671)).

That year an internal list of thirty significant reactor incidents became
public. The official history presents the report as meant to show safety
improving ([WB: ch. 19][fifty-19]); the Senate investigator who obtained it
remembers it as evidence of concealment ([EXT: Alliance for Nuclear
Accountability][alvarez]). The reactors went down one after another — K in
April, L in June, P in August — over emergency cooling concerns
([WB: ch. 19][fifty-19]). On 30 September 1988 the General Accounting Office
told Senator Glenn's and Representative Synar's committees how P reactor's
start-up had gone ahead despite a reactivity shortfall larger than any the
plant had seen ([DL](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41152603_0340);
[WB: gao.gov][gao-88-68]). Glenn's bill to put the weapons plants under
outside safety oversight was already in hearings
([DL](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41152617_0167)).

### Plant to Site

In April 1989 the Westinghouse Savannah River Company took over from Du Pont
([WB: ch. 19][fifty-19]). The same year the Savannah River Plant became the
Savannah River Site and went on the National Priorities List
([WB: srs.gov 1980s][srs-1980]). GAO's 1989 testimony set the site inside a
complex-wide picture of cleanup and modernization problems
([DL](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41152607_0169)).

DOE still meant to restart. In February 1991 it planned K for that summer
and L for 1992, at $3.2 billion for restart and operations
([WB: GAO RCED-91-95][gao-91-95]), and issued safety evaluations for K
([EXT: OSTI 5625511][osti-k-ser]). In December 1991 tritium from K reactor
reached the Savannah River ([EXT: OSTI 5281815][osti-k-tritium];
[EXT: OSTI 10179303][osti-k-dose]). In 1992 K went critical for a test run
and was then shut down permanently ([WB: srs.gov 1990s][srs-1990]). Bomb
making at Savannah River was over.

What it had released was reconstructed later. The CDC's dose reconstruction
counted about 3,000 release incidents involving tritium between 1954 and 1992
([WB: CDC Phase II][cdc-phase2]); cdc.gov now refuses automated requests, and
the reports are reachable in Wayback.

## Act 3. After the bombs, 1992–

### A cleanup site

The official history marks the turn plainly: the mission became environmental
management, not production ([WB: *SRS at Fifty* ch. 20][fifty-20]). DOE
priced the complex-wide legacy in *Estimating the Cold War Mortgage*
([IA](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_ADA294172)) and traced each waste
stream to the process that made it in *Linking Legacies*
([IA](https://archive.org/details/linkinglegaciesc00unit)). In October 2003
the last spent fuel left the receiving basin, closing what the site called a
forty-year era ([WB][rbof]).

### The tanks

The largest legacy was liquid: about 36 million gallons in 49 tanks, roughly
421 million curies ([WB: DWPF fact sheet][dwpf-fact]). The Defense Waste
Processing Facility, built from 1983 and troubled before it ran
([IA: GAO 1992](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_ADA298946)), began turning
that waste into glass on 12 March 1996 ([WB][dwpf-30]). It poured its
4,000th canister on 31 December 2015, of about 8,000 expected
([WB][dwpf-20]), and had passed 4,500 by its thirtieth anniversary in 2026
([WB][dwpf-30]).

Tanks 17 and 20 were closed in 1997, the first in the nation
([WB][tanks-18-19]). What could be left in the ground was settled by Section
3116 of the 2005 defense authorization act
([EXT: govinfo][pl-108-375]), which Senator Graham said he wrote and which
passed by a single vote ([WB][tanks-18-19]), and which the National Academies
reviewed for Savannah River
([IA](https://archive.org/details/tankwastesplanne0000comm)). Tanks 18 and 19
followed in 2012 ([WB][tanks-18-19]), and in May 2026 Tank 14 became the
eighth old-style tank to reach the end of waste removal in under two years
([WB][tank-14]).

### Sealing the reactors

In March 2008 the site put up South Carolina historic markers for P and R,
the first inside the fence ([WB][pr-markers]). Then Recovery Act money
arrived. Deactivation of the R reactor building was cleared in March 2009
([WB][arra-r]); the reactors were sealed in place between October 2009 and
September 2011 ([EXT: OSTI 1032952][osti-isd]), five years ahead of the
schedule without the stimulus ([WB][pr-closure]). The release marking the end
of that work is gone from srs.gov; the Wayback capture is the copy
([WB][pr-closure]).

### Plutonium: MOX and pits

Congress spent a decade on turning surplus plutonium into reactor fuel at
Savannah River ([DL](https://archive.org/details/gov.gpo.fdsys.CHRG-109hhrg33597);
[DL](https://archive.org/details/gov.gpo.fdsys.CHRG-114hhrg97197)). DOE's
2016 baseline put the mixed-oxide plant at $17.17 billion, finished in 2048
— a document since removed from the watchdog site that posted it
([WB: SRS Watch][mox-baseline]). South Carolina sued to hold the government
to its promises ([WB: complaint][sc-complaint]), and The Post and Courier's
source documents on the fight are on archive.org
([IA](https://archive.org/details/5759279-160209-AG-Files-Another-Lawsuit-Over-MOX)).
In May 2018 DOE certified that diluting and disposing of the plutonium would
cost less than half of finishing MOX; the Fourth Circuit lifted the
injunction keeping the project alive on 8 January 2019
([EXT: 4th Cir.][ca4-mox]). MOX workers had already been offered a job fair
in December 2018 ([WB][mox-jobfair]). By August 2019 DOE reported a metric
ton of plutonium removed from the state ([EXT: SC Attorney General][scag]).

The site's weapons work has not ended with it. DOE decided in November 2020
to make plutonium pits at Savannah River ([EXT: Federal Register][fr-pits]),
and SRS Watch and others are challenging the decision in federal court
([DL](https://archive.org/details/gov.uscourts.scd.265178)). Tritium work has
continued throughout ([WB][tritium-2020]).

### The laboratories

The site's laboratory was named the country's newest national laboratory in
2004 ([WB][srnl-2004]); its first web pages were captured that September
([WB][srnl-web]). Both `SRNL.GOV` and `SRS.GOV` have been Archive-It seeds
since July 2023 ([AIT: collection 21601][ait-21601]).

The ecology laboratory fared worse. Its grant was cut 47 percent for 2006,
with layoffs that September ([EXT: UGA][uga-2005]); the next year's funding
fell to $1 million against an expected $4.5 million, which the site's own
Citizens Advisory Board called reprehensible ([EXT: SRS CAB][cab-240]). The
House Science Committee held a hearing on it in July 2007
([DL](https://archive.org/details/gov.gpo.fdsys.CHRG-110hhrg36143); video
[IA](https://archive.org/details/house.hbs.mars.hrs11SCI2318_070719)). SREL's
web page from that summer links to a statement on its budget crisis
([WB][srel-2007]). That statement was never captured.

### Remembering it

The SRS Museum opened in Aiken after a 2014 ribbon-cutting
([WB][museum-2014]); its website no longer resolves, and Wayback is its record
([WB][museum-web]). The site's archaeologists and its Cold War historic
preservation program work with it ([WB][preserve-2020]) — the same program
that collected Ellenton's memories in 1990.
