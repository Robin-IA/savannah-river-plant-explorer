## Chapter 1. Building the bomb plant, 1950–1969

### A site for the hydrogen bomb

In 1950 the Atomic Energy Commission was expanding the nation's production of
material for nuclear weapons, and it asked the Du Pont company to build and
run new reactors to make it. In June, with war begun in Korea, the order grew
to five heavy-water reactors ([WB: *SRS at Fifty* ch. 7][fifty-7]). The
project became the Savannah River Plant, renamed the Savannah River Site in
1989 ([WB: srs.gov 1980s][srs-1980]).

Du Pont surveyed candidate sites and settled on the middle Savannah River in
South Carolina, a tract big enough to hold an exclusion zone around the
reactors ([WB: ch. 7][fifty-7]). DOE's own timeline dates President Truman's
letter to Du Pont to October 20, 1950 ([WB: DOE,
energy.gov/srs][doe-history]).

The announcement came at noon on November 28, 1950: about 250,000 acres in
Aiken and Barnwell counties, to be called the Savannah River Plant, designed,
built and operated by Du Pont ([WB: *SRS at Fifty* ch. 8][fifty-8]). The next
morning it was in the *Atlanta Constitution*
([IA](https://archive.org/details/per_atlanta-constitution_1950-11-29_83_142))
and, by wire, in papers as far away as Victoria, British Columbia
([IA](https://archive.org/details/victoriadailytimes1950-11-29)).

### The towns inside the fence

The land was not empty. Ellenton, with about 600 people, and Dunbarton, with
231, stood inside the boundary. The area also held smaller communities,
among them Meyers Mill, Hawthorne, Robbins, Leigh and Hattieville. Du Pont
moved the boundary to leave out two others, Jackson and Snelling
([WB: ch. 7][fifty-7]). Most of the farms were worked by tenants, and most of
the agricultural workforce was African American ([WB: ch. 7][fifty-7]).

Ellenton was nearly 80 years old when it was taken. It began in 1873 as a
railroad station on the Dunbar family's land, named for nine-year-old Ellen
Dunbar, and was incorporated in 1880. Its post office closed on February 29,
1952, after 79 years ([WB: SRARP, 1996][srarp-ellenton-1]).

It was a local trading center rather than a rich town. Its merchants grew
from 2 in 1873 to 56 in 1950. One family, the Cassels, ran its power plant,
telephone company and bank ([WB: SRARP, 1996][srarp-ellenton-4]). The bank
failed in the Depression, in 1931 ([WB: SRARP][srarp-ellenton-1]). Louise
Cassels later wrote that in 1950 the town wanted anything that would "spur
their lagging economy" ([WB: ch. 7][fifty-7]).

Ellenton also had a violent past. In September 1876, during Reconstruction,
an alleged assault on a white woman led armed white rifle clubs from across
the region to march on the area. They faced Black militia units until federal
troops arrived. Six to seven hundred white men were arrested and charged with
murder or conspiracy, but none was tried ([WB: SRARP][srarp-ellenton-1]).
Congress took testimony the next year, printed under the heading "Ellenton
Massacre" ([IA: Senate testimony, 1877](https://archive.org/details/southcarolinain101phil)).

Dunbarton grew up around its railroad depot in the 1800s and was incorporated
in 1910 ([WB: SRARP, 1993][srarp-dunbarton-2]). Meyers Mill had stores and a
cotton gin, and Leigh had a banana-crate factory that employed about 300
people in 1950 ([WB: ch. 7][fifty-7]).

How many people were moved depends on who is counting. The first press
release spoke of about 1,500 families; early reports said up to 8,000
farmers; the AEC settled on 6,000 people in 1,500 families
([WB: ch. 8][fifty-8]). The Savannah River Archaeological Research Program
later put it at about 5,000 ([WB: SRARP][srarp-towns]).

The process of acquiring the land was run by the Army Corps of Engineers,
with residents of the first area gone by March 15, 1951, and of the second
by June 1. Landowners who disputed their land appraisal went to federal
condemnation proceedings. At least 4,500 graves had to be moved
([WB: ch. 8][fifty-8]). By late 1951 the government was acquiring 265 tracts
in Ellenton alone
([DL](https://archive.org/details/sim_united-states-congress-hearings-prints-and-reports_1951-11-27)).

Owners objected to how their land was bought. A congressional staff report
in late 1951 found Ellenton residents told to be gone by February 1952, while
their property was still unappraised. Each owner was given a single offer at
the appraised value. Those who took the first offer were as much as 20
percent worse off than those who held out. About a hundred owners met twice
to protest the prices.

The Atomic Energy Commission answered that most condemnations came from
defects in title rather than price, though it conceded that juries in seven
contested cases had awarded substantially more than the government's
appraisal
([DL](https://archive.org/details/sim_united-states-congress-hearings-prints-and-reports_1951-11-27)).

The magazine *The Reporter* brought the story to the nation in March 1951,
when it reported on the quiet doom of Ellenton ([IA](https://archive.org/details/sim_reporter_1951-03-06_4_5)).
*Jet* told a different part of it that December: Black families moving
whole houses fifteen miles to New Ellenton, the replacement town
([IA](https://archive.org/details/sim_jet_1951-12-20_1_8)). A 1965 federal
housing study counted about 30 white and 120 Black families relocated near
New Ellenton ([IA](https://archive.org/details/impactoflargeins0000unse)),
and by 1953 Congress was hearing that New Ellenton had been left out of aid
it needed
([DL](https://archive.org/details/sim_united-states-congress-hearings-prints-and-reports_june-16-17-22-and-23-1953)).

The displaced spoke for themselves decades later, in oral histories
collected from 1990 and published as *Memories of Home* for Ellenton and for
Dunbarton and Meyers Mill ([WB: OSTI 272945][osti-ellenton]; [WB: OSTI
10171577][osti-dunbarton]). The program's web pages and the chapter PDFs
survive in Wayback; srarp.org itself is gone ([WB][srarp-towns]; [WB:
Ellenton volume][srarp-ellenton]).

James McBride's life of James Brown touches on the Black sharecroppers
forced out of Ellenton
([IA](https://archive.org/details/killemleavesearc0000mcbr)). Louise
Cassels's *The Unexpected Exodus* (1971), the classic memoir of the leaving,
is cataloged in Open Library, though it has no copy to read or borrow yet
([OL: Open Library][cassels]).

### Building it

According to the Natural Resources Defense Council, construction began on
reactors in 1951 (reactor R in June, P in July, L in October)
([IA](https://archive.org/details/usnuclearwarhead0002unse)). The Bureau of
Labor Statistics followed the work force in a 1952 study
([IA: part I](https://archive.org/details/sim_monthly-labor-review_1952-06_74_6)).
It records Black tenant farmers arriving in Augusta looking for jobs in May
1951. At an NAACP meeting in Aiken that September, speakers accused Du Pont
and an Augusta union of keeping Black workers out of skilled jobs, and said
they had enough evidence to go to court.

Getting hired was a fight of its own. In November 1951 a House labor
subcommittee under Graham Barden held five days of hearings in Augusta on
charges that union membership had become a condition of work
([DL](https://archive.org/details/sim_united-states-congress-hearings-prints-and-reports_november-5-9-1951_13)).

Its report the next year found that Du Pont hired manual labor through a
verbal understanding with the AFL building-trades unions, turning job
seekers away at the gate, and that Atomic Energy Commission officials had
either approved or failed to find out
([DL](https://archive.org/details/sim_united-states-congress-hearings-prints-and-reports_1952_14)).
In November 1952 pickets from an AFL engineers' union nearly stopped
construction, with about 25,000 other workers honoring the line
([IA](https://archive.org/details/sim_business-week_1952-11-15_1211)).

The same system kept Black workers out of the skilled trades. The National
Urban League's field notes, filed with the Barden hearings, record that it
pressed Du Pont in August 1951 over its failure to hire Black electricians,
mechanics, plumbers, welders and ironworkers. Company officials replied that
as long as the unions could supply the workers, they would hire from no other
source. Apart from 116 Black carpenters, they would not say how many Black
workers they employed ([DL](https://archive.org/details/sim_united-states-congress-hearings-prints-and-reports_november-5-9-1951_13)). When Truman created a committee on government
contract compliance that December, *Jet* expected the Savannah River
contractors to be among its first targets
([IA](https://archive.org/details/sim_jet_1951-12-13_1_7)).

Housing the workers was its own crisis. In February 1951 Congress held a
hearing in Aiken. It expected construction to peak around the start of 1953
([DL](https://archive.org/details/sim_united-states-congress-hearings-prints-and-reports_1951-02-28_0)).
A year later, in February 1952, the mayors of New Ellenton and Augusta
testified about dormitories and about Du Pont's part in housing workers
([DL](https://archive.org/details/sim_united-states-congress-hearings-prints-and-reports_1952-02-22_3)).

*House & Home* reported about 4,000 trailers for married workers
([IA](https://archive.org/details/usmodernist-HH-1953-08)). The housing
builder later sued Du Pont in a case that reached the Supreme Court
([DL](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40386417_2698)), and South
Carolina's attempt to tax Du Pont's work on the plant set out what a
government-owned, contractor-operated plant was in law
([DL](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40385001_0003)).

Du Pont wrote its own record of all this, volume by volume, in construction
and design histories now on OSTI ([WB: OSTI 10159481][osti-construction];
[WB: OSTI 10151705][osti-plantwide]). The company's administrative files
are at the Hagley Museum and Library ([WB: DOE finding aid][ohre-admin]).

### Running it

The 305-M test pile was the first reactor on the site to start up, in 1952,
the year heavy-water production began in D Area ([WB: srs.gov
1950s][srs-1950]). R reactor started up in December 1953, P on February
20, 1954, and all five were running by the end of March 1955 ([WB: *SRS
at Fifty* ch. 11][fifty-11]).

The separations plants took the irradiated fuel and targets from the
reactors, dissolved them, and used chemistry to pull out the plutonium and
other products, leaving the radioactive waste behind. F Area was the world's
first full-scale plant to use this method, called Purex, and purified
plutonium; H Area mostly recovered enriched uranium ([WB: ch. 11][fifty-11]).
F Area started in 1954 and H Area in 1955. Radioactive tritium went to the
AEC from 1955, and the basic plant was complete in 1956 ([WB: srs.gov
1950s][srs-1950]; [WB: History Highlights][history1]). The first
shipment of radioactive plutonium is dated December 1954 by one official source and 1955
by others ([WB: ch. 11][fifty-11]; [WB][history1]).

In 1956 the neutrino was confirmed at P reactor. R and the heavy-water
components test reactor shut down in 1964, and L in 1968
([WB: srs.gov 1960s][srs-1960]).

### What it released

The plant's own figures understated its early releases. The CDC's dose
reconstruction found that the radioactive iodine-131 released to the air in
the 1950s, mainly from the separations plants, was about four times what the
site had reported. That was largely because its samplers caught only one
chemical form of the iodine ([WB: CDC dose reconstruction, Phase II ch.
1][cdc-ch1]). The largest tritium releases to the air came in 1957–59 from
the H Area tritium facilities ([WB][cdc-ch1]).

A fuel failure in R reactor in November 1957 sent more than 1,900 curies of
radioactivity into a seepage basin dug only that year. In 1958 three
high-level waste tanks in H Area leaked through their steel walls ([WB: CDC
Phase II ch. 5][cdc-ch5]).

The final report judged the doses to people living nearby small, but found
that a child born in 1955 received much of a lifetime dose from the site in
its first years, from those early iodine releases ([WB: CDC Phase
III][cdc-phase3]).

On March 14, 1955, after a light rain, contamination across the plant set off
the shoe monitors of workers coming in from outside. The plant blamed
fallout from a bomb test in Nevada two days before. In 1985 Bernd Franke and
Robert Alvarez argued that a reactor accident was the likelier source, since
the contamination centered on the K, L and P reactors ([WB: CDC Phase II
ch. 6][cdc-ch6]).

At Senator Thurmond's request, the staff of the Senate Armed Services
Committee investigated and reported in July 1986 that their case was "merely
speculative": the contamination matched fallout from the Teapot Hornet test,
and the plant's own monitoring showed no release
([DL](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41152637_1174)). The CDC's
contractor judged Nevada fallout highly probable, while noting that the full
extent of the deposit would never be known ([WB: CDC Phase II ch.
6][cdc-ch6]).

The workers' side of the reckoning came later still. In 2012 the government
added everyone who had worked at the site for at least 250 days between 1953
and 1972 to the Special Exposure Cohort of the federal occupational illness
compensation program. Their cancer claims no longer turn on reconstructing
each worker's dose ([WB: NIOSH][niosh-srs]).

### Living with the Plant

The plant brought thousands of newcomers to a rural area. At a Senate hearing
in Aiken in February 1952, the AEC's local manager said there would be no
government town like Oak Ridge or Richland; private builders would house the
workers. He counted 3,000 trailers already in private courts, and new
classrooms paid for through the federal Office of Education
([DL: Senate hearing, 1952](https://archive.org/details/sim_united-states-congress-hearings-prints-and-reports_1952-02-22_3)).

Aiken had 7,167 people in 1950 and told the same hearing it expected
metropolitan Aiken to reach 20,000 by 1955. It asked for federal help with
about $2.6 million of the $2.9 million in new water, sewer, police and fire
services it said it needed ([DL](https://archive.org/details/sim_united-states-congress-hearings-prints-and-reports_1952-02-22_3)).

A Labor Department study found more than 10,000 workers had moved into the
area by November 1951. School enrollment rose 69 percent in Williston, some
Barnwell County schools ran double shifts, and under segregation one town put
off building a needed school for Black children while it waited for federal
money ([IA: *Monthly Labor Review*, 1952](https://archive.org/details/sim_monthly-labor-review_1952-09_75_3)).

From 1954 the government paid Aiken, Barnwell and Allendale counties in place
of the property taxes the site's land no longer paid ([WB: DOE, 2008][doe-pilt-2008]).

### SRS and Ecology

Before the reactors ran, the river was surveyed. Ruth Patrick of the Academy
of Natural Sciences made a baseline survey in 1951 ([WB: srs.gov
1950s][srs-1950]), and the Public Health Service sampled the river's water
and its natural radioactivity in 1951–52
([IA](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41159405_0033);
[IA](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41159413_0274)).

AEC officials approached Eugene Odum of the University of Georgia in 1951 to
study the reservation's ecosystems
([IA](https://archive.org/details/bigecologyemerge0000cole);
[IA](https://archive.org/details/isbn_9780820324739)). That work became a
permanent laboratory in 1961 ([WB: srs.gov 1960s][srs-1960]) — the Savannah
River Ecology Laboratory, which dates its own founding to 1951 ([WB:
SREL][srel-radioecology]).

Much of the land had been worked hard. When the government took it, about
two-thirds was forest, most of it already logged, and a third was cropland or
pasture ([IA: SREL scientists, 2002](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_ADA400802)). In 1952 the AEC and
the U.S. Forest Service agreed to manage the land together, and the Forest
Service's first job was planting trees "by the thousands" on old farms
([DL: Forest Service, 1995](https://archive.org/details/savannahriverfor51unit)).

The site and its reactors changed the local streams and watershed. Lower Three Runs Creek was dammed in 1958
to make Par Pond, a cooling reservoir, and the reactors sent hot water down
creeks such as Steel Creek ([IA: L-Reactor environmental impact statement, 1984](https://archive.org/details/SavannahRiverEnvironmentalImpactStatements)).

## Chapter 2. The reactors under scrutiny, 1970–1992

### A plant that measured itself

Through the 1970s the plant reported its releases. Du Pont's semiannual
monitoring summaries were reprinted in the federal *Radiation Data and
Reports*
([DL](https://archive.org/details/sim_radiation-data-and-reports_1970-12_11_12)),
and its annual environmental reports run from 1974 on OSTI ([WB: OSTI
4174736][osti-env-1974]). In 1972 the plant became the first National
Environmental Research Park ([WB: srs.gov 1970s][srs-1970]); see
[SRS and Ecology](#srs-and-ecology).

<img src="img/srl-californium-cask-c1970.jpg" width="1400" height="1136" alt="Three men around a large spherical shipping cask on a flatbed truck marked Radioactive; the man standing on the truck holds a radiation meter against the cask." loading="lazy">
<figcaption>A health physicist makes a final radioactivity check on a shipping cask of californium-252 leaving the Savannah River Laboratory for Texaco&rsquo;s laboratory in Bellaire, Texas, about 1970. <span class="credit">US Department of Energy photograph HD.5A.047, public domain, via <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:HD.5A.047_(10348253655).jpg">Wikimedia Commons</a>.</span></figcaption>
</figure>

The first on-site anti-nuclear protest came in 1979 ([WB: srs.gov
1970s][srs-1970]).

Radioactive tritium was the plant's largest routine release, averaging more than half a
million curies a year to the air in 1974–77 ([WB: OSTI
6206385][osti-tritium-routine]). Single accidents added to it. On May 2, 1974,
some 479,000 curies of tritium gas escaped from a stack ([WB: OSTI
7100365][osti-1974]), a release a Lawrence Livermore report on the national
emergency-response system's tracking of it called a small quantity, not
dangerous to the public ([WB: OSTI 7282426][osti-arac]).

In September 1984 another 43,800 curies, mostly tritium oxide, drifted
toward New Ellenton and Aiken ([WB: OSTI 6417935][osti-1984]), and on July
31, 1987, a further 172,000 curies were released ([WB: OSTI
7130595][osti-1987]).

Downstream, the plant tested the tritium in the drinking water every month at
the Beaufort-Jasper Water Authority in South Carolina and the Port Wentworth
plant in Georgia. It also screened the 865 deer and 8 hogs killed in its 1971
hunt for radioactive cesium-137 before releasing them to hunters
([DL](https://archive.org/details/sim_radiation-data-and-reports_1974-02_15_2)).

The hunts still go on today; see
[Chapter 4](chapter-4.html#the-hunts-go-on).

### L-Reactor comes back

The Reagan-era buildup needed more radioactive plutonium. In 1982 the plant lowered the
Pu-240 content of its product to blend with Hanford's ([WB: *SRS at Fifty*
ch. 19][fifty-19]), and DOE moved to restart L reactor, idle since 1968, on
an environmental assessment rather than a full impact statement ([WB: OSTI
5060401][osti-l-ea]).

<figure class="fig">
<img src="img/srp-glove-boxes-c1972.jpg" width="1400" height="1132" alt="A worker in white coveralls stands at a long row of steel glove boxes with round glove ports and windows." loading="lazy">
<figcaption>Plutonium production at the Atomic Energy Commission&rsquo;s Savannah River Plant, about 1972. <span class="credit">US Department of Energy photograph HD.6D.918, public domain, via <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:HD.6D.918_(10405627974).jpg">Wikimedia Commons</a>.</span></figcaption>
</figure>

The Natural Resources Defense Council sued. On July 15, 1983, the district
court held that restarting L reactor required an environmental impact
statement and ordered DOE to prepare one ([WB: the court's decision][nrdc-vaughn-op]).
It did not halt the restart: a law reporter's summary says the court refused
a preliminary injunction ([WB: Environmental Law Reporter][nrdc-vaughn]),
while the decision set South Carolina's request for one for a later hearing. The
Senate Armed Services Committee had already held a field hearing in North
Augusta that February
([DL](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41152659_0884)).

There Georgia's Senator Mattingly called the Tuscaloosa aquifer under the
site the main source of water for Georgia. He won an assurance from DOE that
burying the plant's high-level waste in the bedrock beneath it would not be
brought up again. The Natural Resources Defense Council's witness pointed to
cesium contamination off the site, just downstream of where Steel Creek
enters the river
([DL](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41152659_0884)).

South Carolina and Georgia had both opposed bedrock disposal
([IA](https://archive.org/details/nuclearimperativ0000cart)). The final EIS
came in May 1984 ([WB: DOE][l-eis]); it and the other impact statements of
the period are gathered in one archive.org upload
([IA](https://archive.org/details/SavannahRiverEnvironmentalImpactStatements)).
L restarted in 1985. C reactor shut down that year, permanently from 1986
([WB: srs.gov 1980s][srs-1980]).

Outside critics read the record differently from the operator. *Deadly Crop
in the Tank Farm* (1986) assessed the high-level waste tanks
([WB: IEER][deadly-crop]); Du Pont published a rebuttal
([WB: OSTI 5881523][osti-dupont-reply]). The *Washington Post* described
aging reactors carrying the buildup, one down for a cracked vessel — a
clipping that survives in the CIA's files
([IA](https://archive.org/details/cia-readingroom-document-cia-rdp88g01117r000401020009-5)).

### Under the ground

What the plant had poured into its seepage basins came back through the
groundwater. Reviewing permit applications in 1981, South Carolina's health
and environment department judged that the volumes sent to the basins
strongly suggested groundwater problems; in 1983 the plant reported that a
well supplying drinking water to its own employees was contaminated, and two
more followed.

The department's deputy commissioner told Senator Glenn's committee in 1987
that plant staff had at first been reluctant to accept the state's
regulatory authority
([DL](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41152617_0148)).

GAO found solvents in the groundwater under the M Area fuel plant at more
than 30,000 times proposed drinking-water standards. Tritium at the
separations areas was more than 2,500 times the standard. DOE answered that
the contamination stayed under federal land and posed no threat to the public
([IA: GAO 1986](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41152607_0232)).

By 1991 the Office of Technology Assessment reported an EPA finding that the
pump-and-treat system meant to clean the M Area plume was not adequately
designed to reach its goal
([DL](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41152852_0090)).

### 1987–1988: the reactors stop

A National Academy of Sciences panel reviewed safety at the defense
production reactors for DOE in 1987
([IA](https://archive.org/details/ERIC_ED299108)). Du Pont announced that
year that it would not stay on as operator ([WB: srs.gov 1980s][srs-1980]).
In February 1988 a Senate subcommittee heard DOE, the plant manager and
Du Pont on why L reactor's power had been cut
([IA](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41152840_0671)).

That year an internal list of thirty significant reactor incidents became
public. The official history presents the report as meant to show safety
improving ([WB: ch. 19][fifty-19]); the Senate investigator who obtained it
remembers it as evidence of concealment ([WB: Alliance for Nuclear
Accountability][alvarez]).

The reactors went down one after another — K in April, L in June, P in
August — over emergency cooling concerns ([WB: ch. 19][fifty-19]). On September
30, 1988, the General Accounting Office told Senator Glenn's and
Representative Synar's committees how P reactor's start-up had gone ahead
despite a reactivity shortfall larger than any the plant had seen
([DL](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41152603_0340); [WB:
gao.gov][gao-88-68]).

Glenn's bill to put the weapons plants under outside safety oversight was
already in hearings
([DL](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41152617_0167)). The next day,
October 1, the *New York Times* put the story on its front page. It reported
severe accidents at all five reactors, kept secret for up to 31 years. They
were documented in a 19-page memorandum from a plant supervisor to his
superiors at Du Pont ([WB: *New York Times*][nyt-881001]). By November the
paper was asking how Congress had missed the signs ([WB: *New York
Times*][nyt-881128]).

### Plant to Site

In April 1989 the Westinghouse Savannah River Company took over from Du Pont
([WB: ch. 19][fifty-19]). The same year the Savannah River Plant became the
Savannah River Site and went on the National Priorities List ([WB: srs.gov
1980s][srs-1980]). GAO's 1989 testimony set the site inside a complex-wide
picture of cleanup and modernization problems
([DL](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41152607_0169)).

That August Senator Glenn argued that DOE's research on the health of its
own workers had been subordinated to production. Senator Kohl called for
removing the apparent conflict of interest, and said he was open to moving
that research to Health and Human Services. The record included a 1988 study
that reported a significant excess of leukemia deaths among hourly Savannah
River workers hired before 1955 ([IA: Senate hearing,
1989](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41152837_0739)). The later dose
reconstruction was run by the CDC ([WB: CDC][cdc-srs-page]).

Studies of the neighbors began at the same time. A July 1990 National
Institutes of Health survey compared cancer deaths in Aiken and Barnwell
counties and Georgia's Burke County with similar counties elsewhere. It found
no excess among children, more leukemia at ages 20 to 39 and slightly more
lung cancer in older adults, and said it did not show cancer excesses tied to
the plant ([DL](https://archive.org/details/cancerinpopulati00jabl_1); [DL](https://archive.org/details/cancerinpopulati00jabl_0)).

DOE still meant to restart. In February 1991 it planned K for that summer
and L for 1992, at $3.2 billion for restart and operations ([WB: GAO
RCED-91-95][gao-91-95]), and issued safety evaluations for K ([WB: OSTI
5625511][osti-k-ser]). In December 1991 tritium from K reactor reached the
Savannah River ([WB: OSTI 5281815][osti-k-tritium]; [WB: OSTI
10179303][osti-k-dose]).

GAO had warned in 1989 that DOE had only begun to address earthquake
weaknesses that had existed at the site for years. It also found that the
seismic review behind P reactor's 1988 restart used a less stringent standard
than the Nuclear Regulatory Commission's ([IA: GAO
1989](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41152645_0571)).

In March 1991 a depression was found on the downstream face of the Par Pond
dam. That summer the site lowered the reservoir about 19 feet. This exposed
some 1,300 acres of cesium-contaminated sediment and sent tritium and cesium
into the river ([WB: OSTI 7247383][osti-parpond]). The exposed bed became a
Superfund interim action ([WB: OSTI 10192315][osti-parpond-cercla]).

A 1998 DOE notice dates the leak to 1992
([DL](https://archive.org/details/sim_federal-register-find_1998-01-28_63_18)).
In 1992 K was started up for a test run and was then shut down permanently
([WB: srs.gov 1990s][srs-1990]). Bomb making at Savannah River was over.

What it had released was reconstructed later. The CDC's dose reconstruction
counted about 3,000 release incidents involving tritium between 1954 and 1992
([WB: CDC Phase II][cdc-phase2]); cdc.gov now refuses automated requests, and
the reports are reachable in Wayback.

### Living with the Plant

The plant was the area's anchor employer. A 1984 Energy Department report put
its permanent workforce at 9,200 in December 1982, most of them working for
Du Pont, with plant workers and their families making up about half of
Aiken's 15,000 people. The same report found that Aiken and Richmond counties
ranked in the top half nationally for income in 1974, while most counties
around the plant ranked in the bottom 11 percent ([IA: DOE, 1984](https://archive.org/details/SavannahRiverEnvironmentalImpactStatements)).

The 1980s buildup brought the workforce to 18,635 by May 1989, and a 1990
DOE study said shutting the K, L and P reactors would cost about 9,600 jobs
([IA: DOE, 1990](https://archive.org/details/SavannahRiverEnvironmentalImpactStatements)). In 1992 South Carolina listed Westinghouse
Savannah River, with 23,000 workers, as its largest manufacturing employer,
and noted that federal cutbacks had begun
([IA: South Carolina Economic Report, 1993](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40706946_0199)).

### SRS and Ecology

In 1972 the site became the first National Environmental Research Park
([WB: srs.gov 1970s][srs-1970]). The ecology laboratory's work on reactor
cooling water began in 1969, once satellite imagery had made the water
temperatures impossible to keep secret ([WB: Ecological Society of
America][esa-thermal]), and became the *Thermal Ecology* symposia
([IA](https://archive.org/details/thermalecologyii0000ther)).

The cooling water did real damage. A 1987 environmental impact statement
found that K reactor's hot water had altered 670 acres of forested wetland
along Pen Branch. C reactor's had altered about 1,147 acres, some 60 percent,
along Four Mile Creek, where open water and marsh had replaced hardwood forest
([IA: DOE, 1987](https://archive.org/details/SavannahRiverEnvironmentalImpactStatements)).
Restarting L reactor meant sending hot water into the Steel Creek swamp again
([DL: Senate hearing, 1983](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA41152659_0884)).

Wildlife was in trouble too. In 1984 the wood stork was listed as endangered,
and the L-Reactor study recorded 102 of them feeding near the Steel Creek
delta the summer before ([IA: DOE, 1984](https://archive.org/details/SavannahRiverEnvironmentalImpactStatements)). In 1985 the site's
red-cockaded woodpeckers were down to four birds; at DOE's request, Forest
Service scientists began carving artificial nest cavities and moving birds in
from other forests ([DL: Forest Service](https://archive.org/details/redcockadedwoodp23unit)).

## Chapter 3. After the bombs, 1992–2020

### A cleanup site

The official history marks the turn plainly: the mission became environmental
management, not production ([WB: *SRS at Fifty* ch. 20][fifty-20]). DOE
priced the complex-wide legacy in *Estimating the Cold War Mortgage*
([IA](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_ADA294172)) and traced each waste
stream to the process that made it in *Linking Legacies*
([IA](https://archive.org/details/linkinglegaciesc00unit)). In October 2003
the last spent fuel left the receiving basin, closing what the site called a
forty-year era ([WB][rbof]).

Spent fuel kept arriving as well as leaving. The United States moved to take
back the fuel it had supplied to research reactors abroad. In 1994 GAO
reported that delays had already led reactors in Belgium and Germany to
reprocess theirs, and that DOE meant to store the first returns at Savannah
River ([IA: GAO 1994](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_ADA344922)).

DOE's 1996 decision accepted about 22,700 fuel elements from 41 countries
over 13 years, most landing at the Charleston Naval Weapons Station, with
the aluminum-based fuel all managed at Savannah River ([DL: *Federal
Register*,
1996](https://archive.org/details/sim_federal-register-find_1996-05-17_61_97)).
South Carolina's governor took DOE to court over it
([DL](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40386015_0538)).

### The tanks

The largest legacy was liquid: about 36 million gallons in 49 tanks, roughly
421 million curies ([WB: DWPF fact sheet][dwpf-fact]). The Defense Waste
Processing Facility, built from 1983 and troubled before it ran
([IA: GAO 1992](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_ADA298946)), began turning
that waste into glass on March 12, 1996 ([WB][dwpf-30]).

Getting the waste into the glass plant was harder. In 1983 DOE chose a
process called In-Tank Precipitation to pull radioactive cesium out of the
tanks' salt. It was expected to cost $32 million and be finished by 1988.
After about a decade and almost half a billion dollars, it was abandoned in
1998 because it gave off large amounts of benzene ([DL: House hearing,
2000](https://archive.org/details/gov.gpo.fdsys.CHRG-106hhrg64703); [WB:
GAO][gao-itp]).

Its replacement, the Salt Waste Processing Facility, was to be built by 2008
for $900 million; in 2015 DOE's Inspector General reported about $1.4
billion in cost growth and a start no earlier than December 2018 ([WB: DOE
Inspector General][oig-swpf]). It poured its 4,000th canister on December 31,
2015, of about 8,000 expected ([WB][dwpf-20]).

Tanks 17 and 20 were closed in 1997, the first in the nation
([WB][tanks-18-19]). What could be left in the ground was settled by Section
3116 of the 2005 defense authorization act ([WB: govinfo][pl-108-375]),
which Senator Graham said he wrote and which passed by a single vote
([WB][tanks-18-19]), and which the National Academies reviewed for Savannah
River ([IA](https://archive.org/details/tankwastesplanne0000comm)).

Tanks 18 and 19 followed in 2012 ([WB][tanks-18-19]).

In 2018 and 2019 DOE reinterpreted "high-level waste" to turn on how
radioactive a waste is rather than where it came from, revising the change
after objections from the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, members of Congress
and states. Savannah River was its first test: in 2020 DOE decided to send
grouted wastewater from the glass plant to a commercial disposal site in
Andrews, Texas, beginning with 8 gallons
([WB: *Federal Register*][fr-hlw-ea]; [WB: *Federal Register*][fr-hlw-fonsi]).

### Sealing the reactors

In March 2008 the site put up South Carolina historic markers for P and R,
the first inside the fence ([WB][pr-markers]). Then Recovery Act money
arrived.

### Plutonium: MOX and pits

South Carolina's fight over radioactive plutonium began before MOX. In April 2002 DOE
decided to store surplus plutonium from Rocky Flats at Savannah River.
Governor Jim Hodges sued, then issued an executive order banning plutonium
from the state's roads, and in June a federal court barred him from
interfering with the shipments; the Fourth Circuit found that DOE had not
broken environmental law ([DL: *Hodges v. Abraham*](https://archive.org/details/micro_IA40386015_0538)).
He had vowed to lie in the road to stop them
([IA](https://archive.org/details/nucleartoxicwast0000kall)).

Congress spent a decade on turning surplus plutonium into reactor fuel at
Savannah River
([DL](https://archive.org/details/gov.gpo.fdsys.CHRG-109hhrg33597);
[DL](https://archive.org/details/gov.gpo.fdsys.CHRG-114hhrg97197)). DOE's
2016 baseline put the mixed-oxide plant at $17.17 billion, finished in 2048
— a document since removed from the watchdog site that posted it ([WB: SRS
Watch][mox-baseline]).

South Carolina sued to hold the government to its promises ([WB:
complaint][sc-complaint]), and The Post and Courier's source documents on
the fight are on archive.org
([IA](https://archive.org/details/5759279-160209-AG-Files-Another-Lawsuit-Over-MOX)).
In May 2018 DOE certified that diluting and disposing of the plutonium would
cost less than half of finishing MOX; the Fourth Circuit lifted the
injunction keeping the project alive on January 8, 2019 ([WB: 4th
Cir.][ca4-mox]).

MOX workers had already been offered a job fair in December 2018
([WB][mox-jobfair]). By August 2019 DOE reported a metric ton of plutonium
removed from the state ([WB: SC Attorney General][scag]). Half of that ton
had gone, secretly, to Nevada.

DOE announced in August 2018 that it planned to send up to a metric ton
there; Nevada sued, and learned through the suit that DOE had already
shipped half of it while seemingly negotiating in good faith. In a 2020
settlement DOE agreed to remove that half ton from Nevada by the end of 2026
and to send no more ([WB: Nevada Attorney General][nv-ag]).

The site was also offered other countries' material. In 2014 DOE began
assessing whether to take Germany's spent pebble-bed reactor fuel: graphite
spheres containing American-supplied highly enriched uranium ([WB: *Federal
Register*][fr-german]). The site's own Citizens Advisory Board voted against
it in 2017 ([WB: CAB][cab-350]). By 2022 the German state concerned had
chosen to store it at home instead ([WB: SRS Watch][srswatch-german]).

Plutonium from a Japanese research assembly did come, through Charleston in
2016 ([WB: SRS Watch][srswatch-japan]). In 2021 NNSA dropped its plan to send
it on to New Mexico. It decided instead to process the plutonium at Savannah
River and store it there until a geologic repository exists ([WB: *Federal
Register*][fr-japan]).

The site's weapons work never fully ended. Work with radioactive tritium continued
throughout ([WB][tritium-2020]).

In 2019 the Defense Nuclear Facilities Safety Board warned that a fire,
crane drop or explosion in the tritium facilities could cause acute
radiation sickness or death in a significant number of people ([WB: *Federal
Register*][fr-dnfsb]). NNSA's administrator replied, "I do not accept
Recommendation 2019-2" ([WB: *Federal Register*][fr-dnfsb-reply]).

### Sick workers

Compensation for the plant's sick workers came slowly. At a 2003 House
hearing Representative Charlie Norwood of Georgia said more than 300 of his
constituents had filed claims over work at the site, and only a handful had
a decision. The building trades' statement said Savannah River workers were
treated unfairly under the program ([DL: House hearing,
2003](https://archive.org/details/gov.gpo.fdsys.CHRG-108hhrg90178)).

The 2012 class that spared early workers individual dose reconstruction was
extended in 2021 to subcontracted construction workers through 1990 ([WB:
*Federal Register*][fr-sec-2021]) and in 2026 through 1993 ([EXT: *Federal
Register*][fr-sec-2026]).

Studies of the workers kept finding leukemia. A 1995 Oak Ridge study found
that the site's workers died at lower rates than the general public but had
significantly more leukemia ([WB: site epidemiology summary][srs-epi-2002]).
In 2007 NIOSH-funded researchers following 18,883 workers hired from 1950 to
1986 found leukemia risk rising with recorded radiation dose, and excess
cancer of the lung lining, often linked to asbestos ([WB: NIOSH][niosh-mortality]).

The records behind the claims had gaps. In 2007 NIOSH found that the site
had not sent neutron radiation data for some claims ([WB: NIOSH][niosh-per19]).
That November a petition asked to add every worker from 1950 to 2007 to the
Special Exposure Cohort; the special classes for the site came from it
([WB: NIOSH][niosh-srs-2026]).

### The neighbors and the river

The Medical University of South Carolina ran a cancer and birth-defects
registry from 1991 to 1995 for the counties around the site and down the
river to Savannah. Some cancers were elevated, among them cervical cancer in
Black women and esophageal cancer in Black men, but officials saw no sign
they were related to the site ([WB: 2002 summary][srs-epi-2002]).

In 2007 the federal Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry (ATSDR)
found "no apparent public health hazard" from groundwater or surface water
leaving the site ([WB: ATSDR][atsdr-2007]). An advocacy group, the Radiation
and Public Health Project, disagreed in 2012, reporting that 46 of 48
radiation-sensitive disease measures in five nearby counties had risen
faster than national rates ([WB: advocacy report][rphp-2012]).

Fishing on the site itself and some surrounding areas is prohibited, with
warning signs posted. A 1997 survey of 258 people fishing the river near the site found
they ate about 3.2 pounds of fish a month, Black men nearly twice as much as
white men, and that families shared the catch with young children
([WB: ATSDR][atsdr-2012]).

In 2012 ATSDR found no harmful radiation from eating local crops, livestock
or game. It warned that eating a lot of largemouth bass, bowfin and catfish
could raise mercury risks, especially for pregnant women and children.
Mercury is high upstream of the site too, and ATSDR said the site's share "is
not known" ([WB: ATSDR][atsdr-2012]).

### Living with the Plant

Then came the cuts. The site employed 23,351 people in 1992, about 10 percent
of all jobs in six surrounding counties, with a payroll above $1.1 billion,
and a 1995 DOE study warned that budget cuts could remove up to 4,200 more
jobs ([IA: DOE, 1995](https://archive.org/details/SavannahRiverEnvironmentalImpactStatements)).

A study for the SRS Community Reuse
Organization, a regional development group with an interest in the site's
value, counted 25,180 workers in September 1991 and 10,967 in September 2010
([IA: study for the SRS Community Reuse Organization, 2011](https://archive.org/details/5750328-USC-Study-on-Economic-Impact-of-Savannah-River)).
The counts differ by source.

The government's payments in place of taxes became a fixture. In 2008 DOE
raised them from $3.5 million to $6.2 million a year ([WB: DOE][doe-pilt-2008]).
GAO later found that Barnwell County received about $4.5 million of the
$6.2 million paid in 2013, and that the land values behind the payments had
been set in 1988 ([WB: GAO, 2019][gao-pilt-2019]).

### SRS and Ecology

The land recovered. By 1994 the Forest Service reported the site's
red-cockaded woodpeckers had grown from 4 to 77 birds in nine years; it was
restoring drained Carolina bays, round wetlands found across the region, and
planting longleaf pine ([DL: Forest Service, 1995](https://archive.org/details/savannahriverfor51unit)).

Ecology laboratory scientists wrote in 2002 that cooling water had "destroyed
the floodplain forests" along several streams, and that after the reactors
shut down they were studying how to replant Pen Branch and Four Mile Creek.
The site then held 30 set-aside research areas of 14,005 acres, and 152
woodpeckers by 1999 ([IA: SREL scientists, 2002](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_ADA400802)).

### The laboratories

The site's laboratory was named the country's newest national laboratory in
2004 ([WB][srnl-2004]).

The ecology laboratory fared worse. Its grant was cut 47 percent for 2006,
with layoffs that September ([WB: UGA][uga-2005]); the next year's funding
fell to $1 million against an expected $4.5 million, which the site's own
Citizens Advisory Board called reprehensible ([WB: SRS CAB][cab-240]).

The House Science Committee held a hearing on it in July 2007
([DL](https://archive.org/details/gov.gpo.fdsys.CHRG-110hhrg36143); video
[IA](https://archive.org/details/house.hbs.mars.hrs11SCI2318_070719)).
SREL's web page from that summer mentions a statement on a budget crisis
([WB][srel-2007]) but the actual statement has been lost.

### Remembering it

The SRS Museum opened in Aiken after a 2014 ribbon-cutting
([WB][museum-2014]); its website is dead but the Wayback Machine has the
record ([WB][museum-web]). The site's archaeologists and its Cold War historic
preservation program work with it ([WB][preserve-2020]) — the same program
that collected Ellenton's memories in 1990.

The federal record of historic engineering holds one part of the plant in
detail. The Historic American Engineering Record documented the Physics
Assembly Laboratory in M Area in 107 photographs and 123 data pages. It was
home to the zero-power Process Development Pile, which set the physics for
plutonium and tritium production in the five reactors
([WB: Library of Congress, HAER SC-43][haer-sc43]).

## Chapter 4. What's happening now, 2020 to today

### Making pits again

The site's weapons work is growing again. DOE decided in November 2020 to
make pits, the radioactive plutonium cores of nuclear weapons, at Savannah River
([WB: Federal Register][fr-pits]), and SRS Watch and others are challenging
the decision in federal court
([DL](https://archive.org/details/gov.uscourts.scd.265178)). The plant is
being built by repurposing an unfinished concrete structure on the site, and
in 2024 its builders were still promising it by 2032 ([WB: SRS][srs-srppf-2024]).

In 2022 NNSA told Congress the site would not reach 50 pits a year until
sometime between 2032 and 2035, not the 2030 target
([IA: Congressional Research Service](https://archive.org/details/crs-r44442.31)).
In January 2023 GAO found that NNSA had no complete schedule or full cost
estimate for pit production, and counted at least $18 billion to $24 billion
in potential costs across its two pit sites ([WB: GAO][gao-pits-2023]).

By February 2026 GAO reported the main building's possible cost at over
$22 billion, up from a preliminary $6.9 billion to $11.1 billion, with
completion in September 2035 at the earliest ([WB: GAO 2026][gao-pits-2026]).
NNSA's own review of 2025 said its contractor had "underperformed" on the
project and been "negligent in reporting project slips"
([WB: NNSA][nnsa-fy25]). On October 15, 2026, DOE approved the plant's
baseline and authorized full construction ([WB: NNSA][nnsa-srppf]).

Safety for the workers inside is a live question. In August 2023 the Defense
Nuclear Facilities Safety Board warned that the planned controls "may not
adequately protect facility workers" handling plutonium in glove boxes
([WB: safety board][dnfsb-2023]). In May 2025 the board said NNSA's upgrades
were "a significant improvement to safety" and resolved its concerns
([WB: safety board][dnfsb-2025]).

### Moving plutonium out

South Carolina won a deal over the plutonium shipped in for the MOX plant.
On August 31, 2020, DOE agreed to pay the state $600 million and to remove
9.5 metric tons of plutonium by 2037; without the deal, DOE expected removal
to take until 2049 ([WB: DOE][doe-sc-2020]).

Removal works by "dilute and dispose": plutonium is mixed with other
material, packed in drums and shipped to the Waste Isolation Pilot Plant in
New Mexico. The site expects tens of thousands of drums over three decades
([WB: SRS][srs-dilute-2024]). In 2025 NNSA said its contractor had beaten its
targets for diluting plutonium and for shipments ([WB: NNSA][nnsa-fy25]).

Then the policy changed. A May 2025 executive order told DOE to halt dilute
and dispose, except what it owes South Carolina, and to offer surplus
plutonium to industry as reactor fuel ([WB: Federal Register][eo-14302]). In
March 2026 DOE said it would restart HB Line, a plutonium processing plant at
H Canyon shut down a few years earlier, to recycle plutonium into reactor fuel
([WB: American Nuclear Society][ans-hbline]).

### Emptying the tanks

A new contractor, Savannah River Mission Completion, took over the liquid
waste in February 2022 ([WB: DOE][doe-srmc-2022]), and DOE set a goal of
closing both tank farms by 2037 ([WB: DOE][doe-srmc-2023]). The Salt Waste
Processing Facility, years late, began radioactive operation in fall 2020
and passed 10 million gallons by November 2024 ([WB: SRS][srs-swpf-2024]).

The glass plant passed 4,500 canisters by its thirtieth anniversary in 2026
([WB][dwpf-30]), and in May 2026 Tank 14 became the eighth old-style tank to
reach the end of waste removal in under two years ([WB][tank-14]). Still,
only 8 of the 51 tanks are formally closed, and about 32 million gallons of
waste remain in the other 43 ([WB: DOE][doe-tanks-2026]).

H Canyon, the site's chemical separations plant, was set to wind down. In
2022 DOE decided to dissolve its remaining spent fuel without recovering the
uranium, emptying L Basin by 2033 instead of 2060
([WB: SRS][srs-abd-2022]). In February 2026 DOE reversed course and restored
uranium recovery, so H Canyon can make fuel for advanced reactors
([WB: DOE][doe-hcanyon-2026]). The site's Citizens Advisory Board has asked
for briefings on what the restart means for the environment
([EXT: SRS Citizens Advisory Board][cab-387]).

### Who runs the site

The laboratory now stands on its own. In December 2020 DOE gave Savannah
River National Laboratory a separate contract, run by Battelle Savannah River
Alliance ([WB: DOE][doe-srnl-2020]). On October 1, 2024, NNSA, the weapons
agency, became the site's landlord, with the cleanup office as its tenant;
the site then had a budget of about $3.8 billion a year and about 13,400
workers ([WB: SRS][srs-landlord-2024]).

Worker safety is still in the news. In April 2024 DOE proposed a $127,440
penalty against the main contractor after a worker lost two fingers in 2022
([WB: DOE][doe-penalty-2024]), and in August 2026 NNSA cited it again over a
thumb amputation in 2025 ([WB: DOE][ea-2026]).

On October 28, 2026, a fire at Savannah River National Laboratory led the
site to declare a Site Area Emergency and close its gates to incoming
traffic. The emergency ended that night, and the site said surveys found "no
detectable levels of contamination" ([WB: SRS][srs-fire-2026]). Its own
releases give two different times for when the emergency was declared
([EXT: SRS][srs-fire-2]).

### Sick workers and the river today

Payments to the site's sick workers keep growing. Labor Department figures
show $271 million in compensation and medical bills by November 2008
([WB: Labor Department][dol-2008]), $1.02 billion by December 2016
([WB][dol-2016]) and $2.23 billion for 13,090 workers by April 2024
([WB][dol-2024]). The current page lists about $3.06 billion for about 15,400
workers ([WB: Labor Department][dol-now]).

South Carolina still advises people not to eat bowfin from the river below
Stevens Creek, and notes that some fish contain radioactive cesium-137 and
strontium-90 ([WB: South Carolina advisory][sc-fish-2026]). Georgia judges
its mercury limits protective enough that it sets none for radioactivity
([WB: Georgia guide][ga-fish-2023]). The site estimated that someone eating
fish from its creek mouths in 2024 would get at most about 0.24 millirem of
radiation, all from cesium-137 ([WB: SRS][srs-dose-2024]).

### Living with the Plant

The payments still matter to the neighbors. When the 2022 federal budget
request cut $5.7 million from them in 2021, Representative Jim Clyburn asked
for the money back, and a Barnwell County council member called the payment
"sort of like the sacred cow" ([WB: *Aiken Standard*, 2021][aiken-pilt-2021]).
South Carolina's 2022–23 budget spent the $525 million from the plutonium
settlement, including $105 million for new schools for Williston and
Blackville ([WB: South Carolina budget, 2022][sc-budget-2022]).

Site workers still give to local charities. The contractors reported raising
$1,488,038 for United Way in the 2023 campaign ([WB: SRS release][srs-uw-2024]),
and the United Way of Allendale, Bamberg and Barnwell counties called the
workers' gifts "a significant portion" of its budget
([WB: contractor release, 2026][srns-uw-2026]). Both are the contractors'
own announcements.

### SRS and Ecology

The woodpeckers kept coming back. The Forest Service counted 150 active
red-cockaded woodpecker groups in 2021, up from 3 in 1985, crediting thinning
and prescribed fire ([WB: Forest Service, 2021][usfs-2021]).

The ecology laboratory restored wetlands for the rare gopher frog in 2021
([WB: DOE][doe-gopher-2021]), and in 2023 released the young of box turtles
seized from illegal collectors ([WB: DOE][doe-turtles-2023]).

### The hunts go on

The site's deer hunts, open to the public every autumn since 1965, are still
held ([WB: SRS, October 2026][srs-hunt-2026]).

Every deer and hog killed is still taken to a check station and tested for
radioactive cesium-137 before the hunter can keep it. The site's limit is a
radiation dose of 22 millirem a year to the hunter
([WB: SRS, October 2026][srs-hunt-2026]). Earlier rules said no animal is
released if its radioactive cesium would put the hunter over the site's yearly
radiation limit ([WB: srs.gov hunt rules][srs-hunt-rules]).
