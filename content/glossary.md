<h2 id="the-plant">The plant and how it worked</h2>
<dl class="glossary">
  <dt id="savannah-river-plant">Savannah River Plant, Savannah River Site (SRS)</dt><dd>The same place under two names. It was the Savannah River Plant from 1950 and was renamed the Savannah River Site in 1989.</dd>
  <dt id="area">Area (F Area, H Area, M Area, D Area and so on)</dt><dd>The site was laid out in separate lettered areas, each with one job: the five reactors (C, K, L, P and R), the separations plants (F and H), fuel and target making (M), heavy-water production (D) and others.</dd>
  <dt id="reactor">Production reactor</dt><dd>A reactor built to make materials for weapons rather than electricity. Uranium fuel and specially made targets were put in the reactor, and the particles given off by splitting uranium turned the targets into plutonium or tritium.</dd>
  <dt id="target">Fuel and targets</dt><dd>The fuel was the uranium that kept the reactor running. The targets were the pieces put in the reactor to be changed into the product, such as plutonium or tritium.</dd>
  <dt id="start-up">Start up (go critical)</dt><dd>A reactor starts up, or &ldquo;goes critical&rdquo;, when splitting atoms first keeps itself going as a steady chain reaction. It is the point at which a reactor is running.</dd>
  <dt id="test-pile">Test pile</dt><dd>A small, very low-power reactor used for experiments and measurements, not for making anything. The 305-M test pile was the first reactor on the site to start up.</dd>
  <dt id="heavy-water">Heavy water</dt><dd>Water whose hydrogen atoms carry an extra particle, making it slightly heavier. The Savannah River reactors used it to slow down and control the chain reaction and to carry heat away. It was made on the site, in D Area.</dd>
  <dt id="disassembly-basin">Disassembly basin</dt><dd>A deep pool of water beside each reactor, where used fuel and targets were taken apart and cooled before going to the separations plants.</dd>
  <dt id="separations">Separations, separations plant</dt><dd>Taking the used fuel and targets from the reactors, dissolving them, and using chemistry to pull out the plutonium and other products, leaving the radioactive waste behind.</dd>
  <dt id="canyon">Canyon</dt><dd>The name for the long, tall, heavily shielded separations buildings in F and H Areas. The work inside was done by remote control because of the radiation.</dd>
  <dt id="purex">Purex</dt><dd>The chemical process used in the separations plants to recover plutonium and uranium. F Area was the world's first full-scale plant to use it.</dd>
  <dt id="cooling-water">Cooling water</dt><dd>River water pumped through the reactors to carry away their heat. It came out hot and ran down the site's creeks or into its reservoirs, Par Pond and L Lake.</dd>
</dl>

<h2 id="materials">Materials and radiation</h2>
<dl class="glossary">
  <dt id="radioactive">Radioactive, radioactivity</dt><dd>Some kinds of atoms are unstable and break down, giving off energy as radiation. A material made of them is radioactive. Radiation can damage living cells, which is why releases from the plant mattered.</dd>
  <dt id="half-life">Half-life</dt><dd>The time it takes for half of a radioactive substance to break down. Some last seconds; others last thousands of years.</dd>
  <dt id="uranium">Uranium, enriched uranium</dt><dd>A heavy radioactive metal mined from the ground and used as reactor fuel. Enriched uranium has had more of the easily split kind of uranium added, which makes it a stronger fuel.</dd>
  <dt id="plutonium">Plutonium</dt><dd>A radioactive metal made in the plant's reactors and used in nuclear weapons.</dd>
  <dt id="tritium">Tritium</dt><dd>A radioactive form of hydrogen, made at the plant for nuclear weapons. It mixes easily with air and water.</dd>
  <dt id="fission-products">Cesium-137, iodine-131, strontium-90</dt><dd>Radioactive substances produced when uranium is split in a reactor. Cesium-137 settles into soil, mud, plants and animals and lasts for decades.</dd>
  <dt id="pit">Pit</dt><dd>The plutonium core at the center of a nuclear weapon.</dd>
  <dt id="mox">MOX (mixed-oxide fuel)</dt><dd>Reactor fuel made by mixing plutonium with uranium, so that plutonium from weapons could be burned in power plants. The MOX plant at the site was never finished.</dd>
  <dt id="neutrino">Neutrino</dt><dd>A tiny particle with no electric charge that passes through almost everything. Reactors give off huge numbers of them, which made a reactor a good place to look for one.</dd>
  <dt id="curie">Curie</dt><dd>A measure of how much radioactivity a material gives off. A curie is a large amount.</dd>
  <dt id="rem">Rem and millirem</dt><dd>Measures of the radiation dose a person receives. A millirem is a thousandth of a rem.</dd>
</dl>

<h2 id="waste">Waste and cleanup</h2>
<dl class="glossary">
  <dt id="high-level-waste">High-level waste</dt><dd>The most radioactive waste, left over from separations. At the site it is a liquid and sludge stored in large underground tanks.</dd>
  <dt id="glass-plant">Glass plant (Defense Waste Processing Facility)</dt><dd>The plant that mixes high-level waste with melted glass and pours it into steel canisters, so it will not leak while it waits for permanent burial.</dd>
  <dt id="seepage-basin">Seepage basin</dt><dd>An open pit dug in the ground for waste water. The water soaked into the soil, and the radioactive and chemical material it carried reached the groundwater.</dd>
  <dt id="groundwater">Groundwater, aquifer</dt><dd>Water held in the soil and rock underground. An aquifer is a layer that holds enough of it to supply wells.</dd>
  <dt id="superfund">Superfund</dt><dd>The federal program for cleaning up the country's worst contaminated sites. The Savannah River Site is on its list.</dd>
  <dt id="eis">Environmental impact statement (EIS)</dt><dd>A detailed public study a federal agency must write before a major action that could harm the environment, comparing the choices and their effects.</dd>
</dl>

<h2 id="agencies">Agencies and programs</h2>
<dl class="glossary">
  <dt id="aec">Atomic Energy Commission (AEC)</dt><dd>The federal agency that owned and directed the nation's nuclear weapons plants when Savannah River was built. Its work later passed to the Department of Energy.</dd>
  <dt id="doe">Department of Energy (DOE)</dt><dd>The federal department that owns the site today.</dd>
  <dt id="nnsa">National Nuclear Security Administration (NNSA)</dt><dd>The part of DOE in charge of nuclear weapons, including tritium and pit production at the site.</dd>
  <dt id="contractor">Contractor</dt><dd>The company hired to run the site for the government: Du Pont from 1950, then Westinghouse, and later other companies.</dd>
  <dt id="dnfsb">Defense Nuclear Facilities Safety Board</dt><dd>An independent federal board that watches over safety at DOE's nuclear weapons sites and makes recommendations.</dd>
  <dt id="special-exposure-cohort">Special Exposure Cohort</dt><dd>A group of workers under the federal compensation program for sick nuclear workers whose cancer claims can be paid without working out each person's radiation dose.</dd>
  <dt id="payments-in-place-of-taxes">Payments in place of taxes</dt><dd>Money the federal government pays the counties around the site, because its land pays no property taxes.</dd>
</dl>

<h2 id="land">The land and the people</h2>
<dl class="glossary">
  <dt id="condemnation">Condemnation</dt><dd>The legal process by which the government takes private land for public use and a court sets the price.</dd>
  <dt id="tenant-farmer">Tenant farmer</dt><dd>A farmer who works land owned by someone else, paying rent in money or in part of the crop.</dd>
  <dt id="reconstruction">Reconstruction</dt><dd>The years after the Civil War, about 1865 to 1877, when the federal government governed and rebuilt the former Confederate states.</dd>
  <dt id="carolina-bay">Carolina bay</dt><dd>A shallow, oval wetland, many of them found across the region and on the site.</dd>
  <dt id="red-cockaded-woodpecker">Red-cockaded woodpecker</dt><dd>An endangered woodpecker that nests in living pine trees.</dd>
</dl>
