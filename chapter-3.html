A cleanup site

The official history marks the turn plainly: the mission became environmental management, not production (WB: SRS at Fifty ch. 20). DOE priced the complex-wide legacy in Estimating the Cold War Mortgage (IA) and traced each waste stream to the process that made it in Linking Legacies (IA). In October 2003 the last spent fuel left the receiving basin, closing what the site called a forty-year era (WB).

Spent fuel kept arriving as well as leaving. When the United States moved to take back the fuel it had supplied to research reactors abroad, GAO reported in 1994 that delays had already led reactors in Belgium and Germany to reprocess theirs, and that DOE meant to store the first returns at Savannah River (IA: GAO 1994).

DOE's 1996 decision accepted about 22,700 fuel elements from 41 countries over 13 years, most landing at the Charleston Naval Weapons Station, with the aluminum-based fuel all managed at Savannah River (DL: Federal Register, 1996). South Carolina's governor took DOE to court over it (DL).

The tanks

The largest legacy was liquid: about 36 million gallons in 49 tanks, roughly 421 million curies (WB: DWPF fact sheet). The Defense Waste Processing Facility, built from 1983 and troubled before it ran (IA: GAO 1992), began turning that waste into glass on March 12, 1996 (WB).

Getting the waste into the glass plant was harder. The process DOE chose in 1983 to pull radioactive cesium out of the tanks' salt, In-Tank Precipitation, had been expected to cost $32 million and be finished by 1988; after about a decade and almost half a billion dollars it was abandoned in 1998 because it gave off large amounts of benzene (DL: House hearing, 2000; WB: GAO).

Its replacement, the Salt Waste Processing Facility, was to be built by 2008 for $900 million; in 2015 DOE's Inspector General reported about $1.4 billion in cost growth and a start no earlier than December 2018 (WB: DOE Inspector General). It poured its 4,000th canister on December 31, 2015, of about 8,000 expected (WB).

Tanks 17 and 20 were closed in 1997, the first in the nation (WB). What could be left in the ground was settled by Section 3116 of the 2005 defense authorization act (WB: govinfo), which Senator Graham said he wrote and which passed by a single vote (WB), and which the National Academies reviewed for Savannah River (IA).

Tanks 18 and 19 followed in 2012 (WB).

In 2018 and 2019 DOE reinterpreted "high-level waste" to turn on how radioactive a waste is rather than where it came from, revising the change after objections from the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, members of Congress and states. Savannah River was its first test: in 2020 DOE decided to send grouted wastewater from the glass plant to a commercial disposal site in Andrews, Texas, beginning with 8 gallons (WB: Federal Register; WB: Federal Register).

Sealing the reactors

In March 2008 the site put up South Carolina historic markers for P and R, the first inside the fence (WB). Then Recovery Act money arrived.

Deactivation of the R reactor building was cleared in March 2009 (WB); the reactors were sealed in place between October 2009 and September 2011 (WB: OSTI 1032952), five years ahead of the schedule without the stimulus (WB). The release marking the end of that work is gone from srs.gov; the Wayback capture is the copy (WB).

Plutonium: MOX and pits

South Carolina's fight over radioactive plutonium began before MOX. In April 2002 DOE decided to store surplus plutonium from Rocky Flats at Savannah River. Governor Jim Hodges sued, then issued an executive order banning plutonium from the state's roads, and in June a federal court barred him from interfering with the shipments; the Fourth Circuit found that DOE had not broken environmental law (DL: Hodges v. Abraham). He had vowed to lie in the road to stop them (IA).

Congress spent a decade on turning surplus plutonium into reactor fuel at Savannah River (DL; DL). DOE's 2016 baseline put the mixed-oxide plant at $17.17 billion, finished in 2048 — a document since removed from the watchdog site that posted it (WB: SRS Watch).

South Carolina sued to hold the government to its promises (WB: complaint), and The Post and Courier's source documents on the fight are on archive.org (IA). In May 2018 DOE certified that diluting and disposing of the plutonium would cost less than half of finishing MOX; the Fourth Circuit lifted the injunction keeping the project alive on January 8, 2019 (WB: 4th Cir.).

MOX workers had already been offered a job fair in December 2018 (WB). By August 2019 DOE reported a metric ton of plutonium removed from the state (WB: SC Attorney General). Half of that ton had gone, secretly, to Nevada.

DOE announced in August 2018 that it planned to send up to a metric ton there; Nevada sued, and learned through the suit that DOE had already shipped half of it while seemingly negotiating in good faith. In a 2020 settlement DOE agreed to remove that half ton from Nevada by the end of 2026 and to send no more (WB: Nevada Attorney General).

The site was also offered other countries' material. In 2014 DOE began assessing whether to take Germany's spent pebble-bed reactor fuel, graphite spheres containing American-supplied highly enriched uranium (WB: Federal Register); the site's own Citizens Advisory Board voted against it in 2017 (WB: CAB), and by 2022 the German state concerned had chosen to store it at home instead (WB: SRS Watch).

Plutonium from a Japanese research assembly did come, through Charleston in 2016 (WB: SRS Watch), and in 2021 NNSA dropped its plan to send it on to New Mexico, deciding to process it at Savannah River and store it there until a geologic repository exists (WB: Federal Register).

The site's weapons work never fully ended. Work with radioactive tritium continued throughout (WB).

In 2019 the Defense Nuclear Facilities Safety Board warned that a fire, crane drop or explosion in the tritium facilities could cause acute radiation sickness or death in a significant number of people (WB: Federal Register); NNSA's administrator replied, "I do not accept Recommendation 2019-2" (WB: Federal Register).

Sick workers

Compensation for the plant's sick workers came slowly. At a 2003 House hearing Representative Charlie Norwood of Georgia said more than 300 of his constituents had filed claims over work at the site and only a handful had a decision, and the building trades' statement called Savannah River workers treated unfairly under the program (DL: House hearing, 2003).

The 2012 class that spared early workers individual dose reconstruction was extended in 2021 to subcontracted construction workers through 1990 (WB: Federal Register) and in 2026 through 1993 (EXT: Federal Register).

Studies of the workers kept finding leukemia. A 1995 Oak Ridge study found that the site's workers died at lower rates than the general public but had significantly more leukemia (WB: site epidemiology summary). In 2007 NIOSH-funded researchers following 18,883 workers hired from 1950 to 1986 found leukemia risk rising with recorded radiation dose, and excess cancer of the lung lining, often linked to asbestos (WB: NIOSH).

The records behind the claims had gaps. In 2007 NIOSH found that the site had not sent neutron radiation data for some claims (WB: NIOSH). That November a petition asked to add every worker from 1950 to 2007 to the Special Exposure Cohort; the special classes for the site came from it (WB: NIOSH).

The neighbors and the river

The Medical University of South Carolina ran a cancer and birth-defects registry from 1991 to 1995 for the counties around the site and down the river to Savannah. Some cancers were elevated, among them cervical cancer in Black women and esophageal cancer in Black men, but registry officials saw no sign they were related to the site, according to a 2002 summary by the site's contractor (WB: OSTI).

In 2007 the federal Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry (ATSDR) found "no apparent public health hazard" from groundwater or surface water leaving the site (WB: ATSDR). An advocacy group, the Radiation and Public Health Project, disagreed in 2012, reporting that 46 of 48 radiation-sensitive disease measures in five nearby counties had risen faster than national rates (WB: advocacy report).

Fishing on the site itself is generally barred, and the creek mouths are posted. A 1997 survey of 258 people fishing the river near the site found they ate about 3.2 pounds of fish a month, Black men nearly twice as much as white men, and that families shared the catch with young children (WB: ATSDR).

In 2012 ATSDR found no harmful radiation from eating local crops, livestock or game. It warned that eating a lot of largemouth bass, bowfin and catfish could raise mercury risks, especially for pregnant women and children. Mercury is high upstream of the site too, and ATSDR said the site's share "is not known" (WB: ATSDR).

Living with the Plant

Then came the cuts. The site employed 23,351 people in 1992, about 10 percent of all jobs in six surrounding counties, with a payroll above $1.1 billion, and a 1995 DOE study warned that budget cuts could remove up to 4,200 more jobs (IA: DOE, 1995).

A study for the SRS Community Reuse Organization, a regional development group with an interest in the site's value, counted 25,180 workers in September 1991 and 10,967 in September 2010 (IA: study for the SRS Community Reuse Organization, 2011). The counts differ by source.

The government's payments in place of taxes became a fixture. In 2008 DOE raised them from $3.5 million to $6.2 million a year (WB: DOE). GAO later found that Barnwell County received about $4.5 million of the $6.2 million paid in 2013, and that the land values behind the payments had been set in 1988 (WB: GAO, 2019).

SRS and Ecology

The land recovered. By 1994 the Forest Service reported the site's red-cockaded woodpeckers had grown from 4 to 77 birds in nine years; it was restoring drained Carolina bays, round wetlands found across the region, and planting longleaf pine (DL: Forest Service, 1995).

Ecology laboratory scientists wrote in 2002 that cooling water had "destroyed the floodplain forests" along several streams, and that after the reactors shut down they were studying how to replant Pen Branch and Four Mile Creek. The site then held 30 set-aside research areas of 14,005 acres, and 152 woodpeckers by 1999 (IA: SREL scientists, 2002).

The laboratories

The site's laboratory was named the country's newest national laboratory in 2004 (WB).

The ecology laboratory fared worse. Its grant was cut 47 percent for 2006, with layoffs that September (WB: UGA); the next year's funding fell to $1 million against an expected $4.5 million, which the site's own Citizens Advisory Board called reprehensible (WB: SRS CAB).

The House Science Committee held a hearing on it in July 2007 (DL; video IA). SREL's web page from that summer mentions a statement on a budget crisis (WB) but the actual statement has been lost.

Remembering it

The SRS Museum opened in Aiken after a 2014 ribbon-cutting (WB); its website is dead but the Wayback Machine has the record (WB). The site's archaeologists and its Cold War historic preservation program work with it (WB) — the same program that collected Ellenton's memories in 1990.

The federal record of historic engineering holds one part of the plant in detail. The Historic American Engineering Record documented the Physics Assembly Laboratory in M Area, home of the zero-power Process Development Pile that set the physics for plutonium and tritium production in the five reactors, in 107 photographs and 123 data pages (WB: Library of Congress, HAER SC-43).
