A site for the hydrogen bomb

In 1950 the Atomic Energy Commission was expanding the nation's production of material for nuclear weapons, and it asked the Du Pont company to build and run new reactors to make it. In June, with war begun in Korea, the order grew to five heavy-water reactors (WB: SRS at Fifty ch. 7). The project became the Savannah River Plant, renamed the Savannah River Site in 1989 (WB: srs.gov 1980s).

Du Pont surveyed candidate sites and settled on the middle Savannah River in South Carolina, a tract big enough to hold an exclusion zone around the reactors (WB: ch. 7). DOE's own timeline dates President Truman's letter to Du Pont to October 20, 1950 (WB: DOE, energy.gov/srs).

The announcement came at noon on November 28, 1950: about 250,000 acres in Aiken and Barnwell counties, to be called the Savannah River Plant, designed, built and operated by Du Pont (WB: SRS at Fifty ch. 8). The next morning it was in the Atlanta Constitution (IA) and, by wire, in papers as far away as Victoria, British Columbia (IA).

The towns inside the fence

The land was not empty. Ellenton, with about 600 people, and Dunbarton, with 231, stood inside the boundary. The area also held smaller communities, among them Meyers Mill, Hawthorne, Robbins, Leigh and Hattieville. Du Pont moved the boundary to leave out two others, Jackson and Snelling (WB: ch. 7). Most of the farms were worked by tenants, and most of the agricultural workforce was African American (WB: ch. 7).

Ellenton was nearly 80 years old when it was taken. It began in 1873 as a railroad station on the Dunbar family's land, named for nine-year-old Ellen Dunbar, and was incorporated in 1880. Its post office closed on February 29, 1952, after 79 years (WB: SRARP, 1996).

It was a local trading center rather than a rich town. Its merchants grew from 2 in 1873 to 56 in 1950. One family, the Cassels, ran its power plant, telephone company and bank (WB: SRARP, 1996). The bank failed in the Depression, in 1931 (WB: SRARP). Louise Cassels later wrote that in 1950 the town wanted anything that would "spur their lagging economy" (WB: ch. 7).

Ellenton also had a violent past. In September 1876, during Reconstruction, an alleged assault on a white woman led armed white rifle clubs from across the region to march on the area. They faced Black militia units until federal troops arrived. Six to seven hundred white men were arrested and charged with murder or conspiracy, but none was tried (WB: SRARP). Congress took testimony the next year, printed under the heading "Ellenton Massacre" (IA: Senate testimony, 1877).

Dunbarton grew up around its railroad depot in the 1800s and was incorporated in 1910 (WB: SRARP, 1993). Meyers Mill had stores and a cotton gin, and Leigh had a banana-crate factory that employed about 300 people in 1950 (WB: ch. 7).

How many people were moved depends on who is counting. The first press release spoke of about 1,500 families; early reports said up to 8,000 farmers; the AEC settled on 6,000 people in 1,500 families (WB: ch. 8). The Savannah River Archaeological Research Program later put it at about 5,000 (WB: SRARP).

The process of acquiring the land was run by the Army Corps of Engineers, with residents of the first area gone by March 15, 1951, and of the second by June 1. Landowners who disputed their land appraisal went to federal condemnation proceedings. At least 4,500 graves had to be moved (WB: ch. 8). By late 1951 the government was acquiring 265 tracts in Ellenton alone (DL).

Owners objected to how their land was bought. A congressional staff report in late 1951 found Ellenton residents told to be gone by February 1952, while their property was still unappraised. Each owner was given a single offer at the appraised value. Those who took the first offer were as much as 20 percent worse off than those who held out. About a hundred owners met twice to protest the prices.

The Atomic Energy Commission answered that most condemnations came from defects in title rather than price, though it conceded that juries in seven contested cases had awarded substantially more than the government's appraisal (DL).

The magazine The Reporter brought the story to the nation in March 1951, when it reported on the quiet doom of Ellenton (IA). Jet told a different part of it that December: Black families moving whole houses fifteen miles to New Ellenton, the replacement town (IA). A 1965 federal housing study counted about 30 white and 120 Black families relocated near New Ellenton (IA), and by 1953 Congress was hearing that New Ellenton had been left out of aid it needed (DL).

The displaced spoke for themselves decades later, in oral histories collected from 1990 and published as Memories of Home for Ellenton and for Dunbarton and Meyers Mill (WB: OSTI 272945; WB: OSTI 10171577). The program's web pages and the chapter PDFs survive in Wayback; srarp.org itself is gone (WB; WB: Ellenton volume).

James McBride's life of James Brown touches on the Black sharecroppers forced out of Ellenton (IA). Louise Cassels's The Unexpected Exodus (1971), the classic memoir of the leaving, is cataloged in Open Library, though it has no copy to read or borrow yet (OL: Open Library).

Building it

According to the Natural Resources Defense Council, construction began on reactors in 1951 (reactor R in June, P in July, L in October) (IA). The Bureau of Labor Statistics followed the work force in a 1952 study (IA: part I). It records Black tenant farmers arriving in Augusta looking for jobs in May 1951. At an NAACP meeting in Aiken that September, speakers accused Du Pont and an Augusta union of keeping Black workers out of skilled jobs, and said they had enough evidence to go to court.

Getting hired was a fight of its own. In November 1951 a House labor subcommittee under Graham Barden held five days of hearings in Augusta on charges that union membership had become a condition of work (DL).

Its report the next year found that Du Pont hired manual labor through a verbal understanding with the AFL building-trades unions, turning job seekers away at the gate, and that Atomic Energy Commission officials had either approved or failed to find out (DL). In November 1952 pickets from an AFL engineers' union nearly stopped construction, with about 25,000 other workers honoring the line (IA).

The same system kept Black workers out of the skilled trades. The National Urban League's field notes, filed with the Barden hearings, record that it pressed Du Pont in August 1951 over its failure to hire Black electricians, mechanics, plumbers, welders and ironworkers. Company officials replied that as long as the unions could supply the workers, they would hire from no other source. Apart from 116 Black carpenters, they would not say how many Black workers they employed (DL). When Truman created a committee on government contract compliance that December, Jet expected the Savannah River contractors to be among its first targets (IA).

Housing the workers was its own crisis. In February 1951 Congress held a hearing in Aiken. It expected construction to peak around the start of 1953 (DL). A year later, in February 1952, the mayors of New Ellenton and Augusta testified about dormitories and about Du Pont's part in housing workers (DL).

House & Home reported about 4,000 trailers for married workers (IA). The housing builder later sued Du Pont in a case that reached the Supreme Court (DL), and South Carolina's attempt to tax Du Pont's work on the plant set out what a government-owned, contractor-operated plant was in law (DL).

Du Pont wrote its own record of all this, volume by volume, in construction and design histories now on OSTI (WB: OSTI 10159481; WB: OSTI 10151705). The company's administrative files are at the Hagley Museum and Library (WB: DOE finding aid).

Running it

The 305-M test pile was the first reactor on the site to start up, in 1952, the year heavy-water production began in D Area (WB: srs.gov 1950s). R reactor started up in December 1953, P on February 20, 1954, and all five were running by the end of March 1955 (WB: SRS at Fifty ch. 11).

The separations plants took the irradiated fuel and targets from the reactors, dissolved them, and used chemistry to pull out the plutonium and other products, leaving the radioactive waste behind. F Area was the world's first full-scale plant to use this method, called Purex, and purified plutonium; H Area mostly recovered enriched uranium (WB: ch. 11). F Area started in 1954 and H Area in 1955. Radioactive tritium went to the AEC from 1955, and the basic plant was complete in 1956 (WB: srs.gov 1950s; WB: History Highlights). The first shipment of radioactive plutonium is dated December 1954 by one official source and 1955 by others (WB: ch. 11; WB).

In 1956 the neutrino was confirmed at P reactor. R and the heavy-water components test reactor shut down in 1964, and L in 1968 (WB: srs.gov 1960s). The standard history of the reactors is Carlisle and Zenzen's Supplying the Nuclear Arsenal (IA), and the South Carolina State Historic Preservation Office holds a long illustrated study of the five reactor areas (WB: SC SHPO).

What it released

The plant's own figures understated its early releases. The CDC's dose reconstruction found that the radioactive iodine-131 released to the air in the 1950s, mainly from the separations plants, was about four times what the site had reported. That was largely because its samplers caught only one chemical form of the iodine (WB: CDC dose reconstruction, Phase II ch. 1). The largest tritium releases to the air came in 1957–59 from the H Area tritium facilities (WB).

A fuel failure in R reactor in November 1957 sent more than 1,900 curies of radioactivity into a seepage basin dug only that year. In 1958 three high-level waste tanks in H Area leaked through their steel walls (WB: CDC Phase II ch. 5).

The final report judged the doses to people living nearby small, but found that a child born in 1955 received much of a lifetime dose from the site in its first years, from those early iodine releases (WB: CDC Phase III).

On March 14, 1955, after a light rain, contamination across the plant set off the shoe monitors of workers coming in from outside. The plant blamed fallout from a bomb test in Nevada two days before. In 1985 Bernd Franke and Robert Alvarez argued that a reactor accident was the likelier source, since the contamination centered on the K, L and P reactors.

At Senator Thurmond's request, the staff of the Senate Armed Services Committee investigated and reported in July 1986 that their case was "merely speculative": the contamination matched fallout from the Teapot Hornet test, and the plant's own monitoring showed no release (DL). The CDC's contractor judged Nevada fallout highly probable, while noting that the full extent of the deposit would never be known (WB: CDC Phase II ch. 6).

The workers' side of the reckoning came later still. In 2012 the government added everyone who had worked at the site for at least 250 days between 1953 and 1972 to the Special Exposure Cohort of the federal occupational illness compensation program. Their cancer claims no longer turn on reconstructing each worker's dose (WB: NIOSH).

Living with the Plant

The plant brought thousands of newcomers to a rural area. At a Senate hearing in Aiken in February 1952, the AEC's local manager said there would be no government town like Oak Ridge or Richland; private builders would house the workers. He counted 3,000 trailers already in private courts, and new classrooms paid for through the federal Office of Education (DL: Senate hearing, 1952).

Aiken had 7,167 people in 1950 and told the same hearing it expected metropolitan Aiken to reach 20,000 by 1955. It asked for federal help with about $2.6 million of the $2.9 million in new water, sewer, police and fire services it said it needed (DL).

A Labor Department study found more than 10,000 workers had moved into the area by November 1951. School enrollment rose 69 percent in Williston, some Barnwell County schools ran double shifts, and under segregation one town put off building a needed school for Black children while it waited for federal money (IA: Monthly Labor Review, 1952).

From 1954 the government paid Aiken, Barnwell and Allendale counties in place of the property taxes the site's land no longer paid (WB: DOE, 2008).

SRS and Ecology

Before the reactors ran, the river was surveyed. Ruth Patrick of the Academy of Natural Sciences made a baseline survey in 1951 (WB: srs.gov 1950s), and the Public Health Service sampled the river's water and its natural radioactivity in 1951–52 (IA; IA).

AEC officials approached Eugene Odum of the University of Georgia in 1951 to study the reservation's ecosystems (IA; IA). That work became a permanent laboratory in 1961 (WB: srs.gov 1960s) — the Savannah River Ecology Laboratory, which dates its own founding to 1951 (WB: SREL).

Much of the land had been worked hard. When the government took it, about two-thirds was forest, most of it already logged, and a third was cropland or pasture (IA: SREL scientists, 2002). In 1952 the AEC and the U.S. Forest Service agreed to manage the land together, and the Forest Service's first job was planting trees "by the thousands" on old farms (DL: Forest Service, 1995).

The reactors changed the streams. Lower Three Runs Creek was dammed in 1958 to make Par Pond, a cooling reservoir, and the reactors sent hot water down creeks such as Steel Creek (IA: L-Reactor environmental impact statement, 1984).
