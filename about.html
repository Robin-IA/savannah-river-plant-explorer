About this explorer

A history of the Savannah River Plant told from the record, with every source labelled by where it is held, so a reader can open it and check.

The project

The Savannah River Plant was built by Du Pont for the Atomic Energy Commission from 1950 on land in Aiken and Barnwell counties, South Carolina, and made plutonium and tritium for nuclear weapons until its reactors were shut down in 1988. Renamed the Savannah River Site in 1989, it has since been a place of clean-up, waste treatment, research and, again, plutonium work.

The story is told in three chapters: building the plant, 1950–1969; the reactors under scrutiny, 1970–1992; and the years after bomb making, 1992 to now. It was written for Democracy's Library, whose aim is to be the most complete source for government documents, and it shows how much of one government site's history sits inside the Internet Archive, and where.

  • IA Internet Archive. An archive.org item outside Democracy's Library: books in the lending library, magazines and newspapers, and government documents in collections such as the DTIC archive and the CIA Reading Room.

  • EXT Held elsewhere. Outside the Internet Archive; the holder is named, most often the Department of Energy's OSTI.

Dates covered

From the Atomic Energy Commission's decision in 1950 to the present. The sources themselves run from 1950 newspapers to web pages captured in 2026.

Topics covered

The choice of site and the towns moved off it, among them Ellenton and Dunbarton; building the plant; the reactors and separations plants; early ecology and the Savannah River Ecology Laboratory; the L-Reactor restart and lawsuit; the 1988 disclosures and shutdowns; the change from Du Pont to Westinghouse; liquid waste and the glass-making plant; sealing the P and R reactors in place; the MOX plant and plutonium pits; the national laboratory; and how the site is remembered.

Controversies

The plant’s history has been argued over from the start, and the story sets out each argument with its sources rather than settling it:

  • The land. Whole towns, Ellenton and Dunbarton among them, were moved off the site from 1951. Most of the farms were worked by tenants, most of the farm workforce was African American, and the count of people displaced is still disputed. Read it in the story.
  • Who got hired. A 1951 House inquiry found that jobs went through the building-trades unions, turning applicants away at the gate, and the National Urban League recorded Black workers kept out of the skilled trades. Read it in the story.
  • What the plant released early on. The CDC’s dose reconstruction found 1950s iodine releases about four times what the site reported; a 1955 contamination blamed on Nevada fallout was later argued to be a reactor accident; workers from 1953 to 1972 were eventually compensated without individual dose reconstruction. Read it in the story.
  • Restarting L reactor. In the 1980s buildup DOE moved to restart L reactor on an environmental assessment; the Natural Resources Defense Council sued, and in 1983 a court ruled that a full impact statement was needed, though it did not stop the restart. Read it in the story.
  • Groundwater and the state. Seepage basins contaminated the groundwater, including employees’ drinking-water wells, and the state regulator said the plant was at first reluctant to accept its authority. Read it in the story.
  • Tritium and Georgia. Large tritium releases in 1974, 1984 and 1987, downstream drinking-water testing, and Georgia’s objection to burying waste above its main aquifer. Read it in the story.
  • Secret reactor accidents. In 1988 an internal list of thirty serious reactor incidents became public. The official history reads it as a record of improving safety, the congressional investigator who obtained it as evidence of concealment, and the New York Times reported accidents kept secret for up to 31 years. Read it in the story.
  • What was released. Tritium from K reactor reached the Savannah River in 1991, and the CDC’s dose reconstruction later counted about 3,000 release incidents involving tritium. Read it in the story.
  • Worker health research and safety. In 1989 senators challenged DOE’s control of research on its own workers’ health; GAO found earthquake weaknesses; the Par Pond dam scare of 1991 exposed contaminated sediment. Read it in the story.
  • Waste left in the ground. What tank waste may stay buried was settled by Section 3116 of the 2005 defense authorization act, which passed by one vote. Read it in the story.
  • The salt waste plants. In-Tank Precipitation was abandoned after almost half a billion dollars, and its replacement ran years late and over budget; DOE later redefined high-level waste to send some to Texas. Read it in the story.
  • Plutonium coming and going. Governor Hodges’s 2002 fight to block plutonium from Rocky Flats; DOE’s secret shipment of half a ton to Nevada; proposals to bring in German and Japanese material; DOE’s rejection of the safety board’s tritium warning. Read it in the story.
  • MOX and pits. DOE’s 2016 baseline put the mixed-oxide fuel plant at $17.17 billion; DOE ended it in 2018–19 while South Carolina sued to hold the government to its promises; DOE’s 2020 decision to make plutonium pits at the site is being challenged in federal court. Read it in the story.
  • Sick workers. Compensation claims moved slowly, and the class of workers spared dose reconstruction was extended in 2021 and 2026. Read it in the story.
  • The ecology laboratory. Funding cuts in 2006 and 2007 brought layoffs, a rebuke from the site’s own Citizens Advisory Board and a House hearing. Read it in the story.

The documentary Building Bombs

Building Bombs (1989), made by Mark Mori and Susan Robinson, is a film about the environmental legacy and social impact of the Savannah River Plant, “the nation’s largest manufacturer of hydrogen bomb materials during the Cold War”, in the words of PBS’s POV WB. It was nominated for the Academy Award for Best Documentary Feature at the 63rd Academy Awards in March 1991 WB: oscars.org.

Public television did not show it at first. In 1992 the cable series Alternative Views presented the film with an interview with its co-director, saying PBS had refused to broadcast it IA: Alternative Views #466. After three years of argument and a campaign Mori organized, an edited version, retitled Building Bombs: The Legacy, aired on POV on 10 August 1993 EXT: Los Angeles Times.

Where the sources disagree

Where sources disagree, the story gives each version with its source rather than choosing. These are the disagreements.

  • How many people were displaced: 5,000 (SRARP), 6,000 in 1,500 families (AEC), up to 8,000 farmers (early reports).
  • First plutonium shipment: 28 December 1954 (SRS at Fifty ch. 11) or 1955 (srs.gov History Highlights, energy.gov).
  • SREL's founding: 1951, the start of Odum's survey (SREL), or 1961, the permanent laboratory (srs.gov 1960s).
  • Why the reactors stopped in 1988: emergency cooling and loss-of-coolant concerns in the official history; the aftermath of Chernobyl and concealment in the investigator's memoir.
  • P reactor's final shutdown: 1988 (SRS, 2008) or 1991 (OSTI, 2012).

What is left out

Sources the story still needs, such as the rest of the New York Times series of autumn 1988 and the “thirty incidents” report itself, are tracked as open issues on GitHub. Facts that could not be traced to a source are left out of the story until one is found.
