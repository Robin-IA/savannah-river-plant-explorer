Sick Workers and the River Today

Payments to the site's sick workers keep growing. Labor Department figures show $271 million in compensation and medical bills by November 2008 (WB: Labor Department), $1.02 billion by December 2016 (WB) and $2.23 billion for 13,090 workers by April 2024 (WB). The current page lists about $3.06 billion for about 15,400 workers (WB: Labor Department).

Most cancer claims still turn on an estimate of each worker's radiation dose, and most of those fail. After NIOSH (National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health) estimated the dose, 2,271 of the site's cancer cases were accepted and 4,025 denied, and another 483 were still waiting (WB: Labor Department). In April 2026 the federal advisory board's Savannah River work group noted that a 1997 contractor check had found 79 percent of special bioassays, the samples that show whether a worker took in radioactive material, were never turned in. Its review of the main contractors' own employees was "still under consideration" (WB: NIOSH advisory board).

Tritium is still most of what the site sends into the river. In 2024 the site released 451 curies of it, and Georgia Power's Vogtle nuclear plant released 1,628. Below the site the river averaged 377 picocuries per liter, against a federal drinking-water limit of 20,000. The site put the most anyone downstream could get from drinking the water at 0.034 millirem a year, about a third of it from the site (WB: SRS).

South Carolina keeps its own watch, paid for by Energy Department grants. Its program samples drinking water that serves about 254,000 customers, and warns downstream water suppliers if tritium in the river passes trigger levels (WB: South Carolina; WB). In 2023 every drinking-water result was below federal limits, but tritium was still "the most abundant radionuclide" found (WB: South Carolina, 2023).
