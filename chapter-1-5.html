Radioactive Releases

The plant's own figures understated its early releases. The CDC's (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's) dose reconstruction found that the radioactive iodine-131 released to the air in the 1950s, mainly from the separations plants, was about four times what the site had reported. That was largely because its samplers caught only one chemical form of the iodine (WB: CDC dose reconstruction, Phase II ch. 1). The largest tritium releases to the air came in 1957–59 from the H Area tritium facilities (WB).

A fuel failure in R reactor in November 1957 sent more than 1,900 curies of radioactivity into a seepage basin dug only that year. In 1958 three high-level waste tanks in H Area leaked through their steel walls (WB: CDC Phase II ch. 5).

The final report judged the doses to people living nearby small, but found that a child born in 1955 received much of a lifetime dose from the site in its first years, from those early iodine releases (WB: CDC Phase III).

On March 14, 1955, after a light rain, contamination across the plant set off the shoe monitors of workers coming in from outside. The plant blamed fallout from a bomb test in Nevada two days before. In 1985 Bernd Franke and Robert Alvarez argued that a reactor accident was the likelier source, since the contamination centered on the K, L and P reactors (WB: CDC Phase II ch. 6).

At Senator Thurmond's request, the staff of the Senate Armed Services Committee investigated and reported in July 1986 that their case was "merely speculative": the contamination matched fallout from Teapot Hornet, an above-ground atomic bomb test at the Nevada Test Site two days before, and the plant's own monitoring showed no release (DL). The CDC's contractor judged Nevada fallout highly probable, while noting that the full extent of the deposit would never be known (WB: CDC Phase II ch. 6).

The workers' side of the reckoning came later still. In 2012 the government added everyone who had worked at the site for at least 250 days between 1953 and 1972 to the Special Exposure Cohort of the federal occupational illness compensation program. Their cancer claims no longer turn on reconstructing each worker's dose (WB: NIOSH).
