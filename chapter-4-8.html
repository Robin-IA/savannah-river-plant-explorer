Looking to the Future

Much of the site's future is already planned. In 2024 it had a budget of about $3.8 billion a year and about 13,400 workers (WB: SRS). Its largest new project, the plant to make plutonium pits, was estimated in 2026 at over $22 billion, finished no sooner than September 2035 (WB: GAO 2026).

DOE (Department of Energy) has promised to remove 9.5 metric tons of plutonium from South Carolina by 2037 (WB: DOE), and its cleanup contractor is to close both tank farms by the same year (WB: DOE). H Canyon is to recover uranium to make fuel for advanced reactors (WB: DOE).

The site's own record is moving. Between May and July 2026, www.srs.gov began sending visitors to a new address, public.srs.gov (WB: May 2026; WB: July 2026). Old links mostly still work, but often land on a broader page rather than the one that was linked, which is why this history cites the Wayback Machine's copies.

Why This Story Matters

The Savannah River Plant shaped the land, the towns and the lives of the people around it, and its story is not finished. It is still cleaning up what it made, while it prepares to make plutonium pits for new weapons. Knowing that history, from the families moved off the land to the workers who fell sick, is part of deciding what comes next.

This story could be told because the Internet Archive keeps the record. All 466 sources it cites are held at the Internet Archive: government documents in Democracy's Library, web pages saved by the Wayback Machine, and books, magazines and reports in its other collections.
