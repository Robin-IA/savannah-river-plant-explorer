Living with the Plant

For the towns around it, the plant was an economic boom with an environmental cost. It brought jobs, newcomers, new schools and federal money. It also brought the radioactive releases described earlier, and the damage to streams and wildlife told in SRS and Ecology. Later chapters follow what that meant for the people who fish the river and drink its water (Chapter 3).

The plant brought thousands of newcomers to a rural area. At a Senate hearing in Aiken in February 1952, the AEC's (Atomic Energy Commission's) local manager said there would be no government town like Oak Ridge or Richland; private builders would house the workers. He counted 3,000 trailers already in private courts, and new classrooms paid for through the federal Office of Education (DL: Senate hearing, 1952).

Aiken had 7,167 people in 1950 and told the same hearing it expected metropolitan Aiken to reach 20,000 by 1955. It asked for federal help with about $2.6 million of the $2.9 million in new water, sewer, police and fire services it said it needed (DL). From 1954 the government paid Aiken, Barnwell and Allendale counties in place of the property taxes the site's land no longer paid (WB: DOE, 2008).

A Labor Department study found more than 10,000 workers had moved into the area by November 1951. School enrollment rose 69 percent in Williston, some Barnwell County schools ran double shifts, and under segregation one town put off building a needed school for Black children while it waited for federal money (IA: Monthly Labor Review, 1952).

The plant also remade the roads around it. Before the plant, South Carolina Highway 28 ran from Augusta through Jackson and past Ellenton toward Allendale (IA: road map, 1948; WB: SRS at Fifty). In early 1951 the state reported that Routes 28 and 64 "will be closed through the AEC area," while a new four-lane highway was built "from North Augusta, S. C., to Route 28 at the outer boundary of the project" (DL: Senate hearing, 1951).

By 1954 that new road carried the number South Carolina Highway 125 (DL: Federal Register, 1954), and by 1955 the plant's newspaper placed a worker's home "in North Augusta on Atomic Road" (WB: SRS at Fifty).

Inside the fence the road was SRP Road A. On July 27, 1967, the Atomic Energy Commission gave the state an easement to run Highway 125 for 18.3 miles through the plant, which is how the public came to drive across it (DL: Federal Register, 1968).

The plant changed the land as well as the towns. SRS and Ecology follows what happened to its forests, streams and wildlife.
